## The argument in one line

Hospitals bought scheduling software that is now being worked around, and the
reason is not the software. It is that nobody owns the configuration after
go-live.

Why this works for their audience: it is about an asset already on the budget,
so it speaks to an executive rather than to a nurse manager, and it offers a
test they can run this week rather than a purchase.

## Structure that fits 600-700 words

1. **Open on the scene, not the thesis** (~80 words). A hospital is paying for
   scheduling software while the real schedule is built somewhere else. Nobody
   decided this; it accumulated. If you have seen this at a specific hospital,
   say so in general terms, because a concrete opening is what makes an editor
   take a Viewpoint.
2. **Name the failure mode** (~120 words). Implementation ends, the rules stop
   matching the unit, and every unmatched rule becomes a manual override.
   Overrides accumulate until building from scratch is faster than correcting
   the draft.
3. **The ownership point, which is the piece's real contribution** (~150 words).
   Enterprise platforms assume a super-user: a staffing coordinator or workforce
   analyst who keeps configuration current. Hospitals under 25 beds do not staff
   that role, so it defaults to a nurse manager who already has a unit to run.
   This is the part no vendor writes about, and it is the reason the same
   platform succeeds at one hospital and is abandoned at another.
4. **Evidence** (~120 words). Pick two, not five. See the bank below.
5. **What an executive can actually do** (~120 words). The override count and
   the parallel-spreadsheet test. Both are measurable from the last three
   posted schedules, and neither requires buying anything.
6. **Close on the decision, not the product** (~60 words). Before renewing any
   scheduling platform, name the person who owns its configuration. If the line
   is blank, the renewal is buying a tool nobody operates.

## Traps, in the order they are likely to bite

1. **Do not name rival products.** The reviewer quotes above are real and public,
   but a piece by a vendor founder that names competitors and criticises them
   reads as competitive positioning, not commentary, whatever the guidelines
   technically permit. Attribute to the pattern instead: "a manager reviewing a
   widely used scheduling platform wrote...". You lose nothing; the argument was
   never about the brand.
2. **The product appears once, in the bio.** Their example format is "Jane Doe
   is CEO of Metropolis Health", so: *Pradeep Pandey is co-founder of
   SimpleScheduleAI.* No description of what we sell, no link in the body, no
   "we help hospitals..." sentence anywhere.
3. **No customer claims.** We are pre-launch with zero customers. Nothing in the
   piece may imply hospitals are using our product or that we have observed
   results at a client. Your Apollo operations background is real and usable;
   SimpleScheduleAI outcomes are not.
4. **Avoid the words that read as marketing.** No "streamline", no "seamless",
   no "solution". They asked for plain conversational English and AP style.
5. **Do not pitch the idea first.** Their guidance is to send the piece, not a
   query. Write it, then email it.
