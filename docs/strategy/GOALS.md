# Customer interview goals — SimpleScheduleAI

SimpleScheduleAI is an AI-native nurse scheduling service for Texas Critical
Access Hospitals of 25 beds or fewer, priced at $1,000 to $1,500 a month by
roster band. The AI builds each cycle's schedule, an internal scheduling team
checks every draft, and the hospital's nurse leader approves. The company is
pre-launch with zero customers. The sale has two heads: the nurse manager, DON,
or CNO who lives with the schedule (the mapped persona in `NEEDS-STACK.md`),
and the administrator or CEO who signs the contract, whose needs ladder has
never been walked.

The interviews these goals drive are the company's first real market contact:
CNO introductions arriving through NAINA. The founder asked specifically for
Texas CNOs but the introductions are coming from all across the US, at
hospitals of many sizes, and the number of calls is unknown. Most interviewees
will therefore be role-matched but not segment-matched to the ideal customer,
which is why G1 exists: every other answer has to be filtered through what
transfers to a 25-bed Texas hospital and what does not.

Decisions on the table, all confirmed by the founder 2026-08-22: settle N9
(the patient-safety-adjacent level in the needs stack that both founder and
author were guessing at; its warning block says to replace our wording with
theirs), which unholds positioning statement P11; walk the administrator/CEO
approval chain that the pricing page and sales motion depend on; design the
path to a first yes for a vendor with no references (the Eager-identity score
in `PROBLEM-SCORE.md` is 0.1 for exactly this reason); decide what to charge
and whose budget line it comes from; and pick channels beyond the NAINA
introduction route.

Standing beliefs these interviews will test, recorded as unvalidated seed
material for the hypotheses step: scheduling takes the nurse leader 8 to 12
hours in a typical week (our own 30+ pre-build interviews, run with nurse
managers generally rather than Texas CAH leaders specifically); callout
coverage, not schedule building, is the biggest time consumer; unfair rotation
drives nurse resentment and eventually turnover; small hospitals lack the
dedicated scheduler and IT staffing that make enterprise tools workable; and
the buyer's alternatives are Excel, a basic tool, agency spend, and doing
nothing.

## Goal questions

**G1.** Which hospital circumstances make scheduling pain acute enough to pay
for, and which parts of the problem change with hospital size? *(holds or
redraws the Texas ≤25-bed ICP line; tells us what from all-US calls transfers
to our buyer)*

**G2.** What actually worries a CNO about a short shift, in her own words?
*(settles N9, the least-evidenced level in the needs stack, and unholds
positioning statement P11)*

**G3.** How does the schedule actually get made at her hospital today — by
whom, with what, and how much of someone's week does it really take? *(tests
the 8-12 hour claim against real hospital leaders; grounds the hero copy)*

**G4.** What has her hospital actually paid to relieve scheduling pain —
software, agency premium, an extra FTE, a consultant? *(our real competition,
including doing nothing; evidence for the budget score)*

**G5.** Whose budget line would a $1,000-1,500/month scheduling service come
from, and who has to approve it? *(pricing page framing; the administrator/CEO
second-head stack; the sales motion)*

**G6.** What contracts, tools, or system mandates currently lock her
scheduling spend, and when do those decisions reopen? *(whether buyers are
even in-market; outreach timing)*

**G7.** What would she need to see before trusting a vendor with zero
customers? *(the first-yes design: guarantee, pilot structure, what
substitutes for references — the weakest score in PROBLEM-SCORE.md)*

**G8.** What event would make fixing scheduling urgent this quarter instead of
someday? *(inciting events; what content and outreach should hook)*

**G9.** What makes her resist or fear changing how scheduling is done — nurse
pushback, IT review, EMR integration expectations, retraining? *(objection
handling; what /how-it-works must answer)*

**G10.** What is a CNO graded on by her CEO or board, and where does nurse
retention sit in that? *(the scoreboard positioning should promise more of;
validates the upper needs-stack levels)*

**G11.** What words does she use for the problem and for our category?
*(positioning-registry vocabulary; the words pages and keywords should carry)*

**G12.** Where does she discover and vet vendors like us — peers,
associations, conferences, directories? *(channel strategy; whether the
NAINA-style association route generalizes)*

Not a goal, but standing instruction for every call: ask at the end whether
the interviewee can introduce us to a Texas Critical Access CNO or
administrator. That is an action item for the interview script, not something
the calls need to teach us.

## Next steps

Write hypotheses: our current best guess of the answer to each goal, mapped by
G-number, so the interviews can confirm or contradict us instead of
confirmation bias quietly filtering what we hear. The strategy files already
hold most of the raw material (the needs stack for G2 and G10, the problem
scorecard for G4 through G7, the positioning file for G11). Then design
open-ended, non-leading interview questions that test each hypothesis, and
interview until the surprises stop. The chain continues with
`asb-interview-hypotheses` against this file.
