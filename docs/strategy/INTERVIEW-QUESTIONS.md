# CNO interview script — SimpleScheduleAI

Tests the hypotheses in `HYPOTHESES.md` (H1-H21) against the goals in
`GOALS.md` (G1-G12). Prepared for the NAINA introductions.

**You will not get through 21 questions in one call.** This is ordered by what
it unblocks, not by hypothesis number. Sections A and B are the must-asks; C is
opportunistic; D closes. A 30-minute call realistically covers A plus two or
three of B.

---

## The four rules that make this work

**1. Never mention a hypothesis, a number, or our product.** The moment you say
"we think it takes 8 to 12 hours", the answer is worthless. If she asks what we
do, answer in one sentence and return the floor to her.

**2. Past behaviour, never opinion.** "What did you do the last time X" beats
"what would you do if X" every time. People predict themselves badly and
describe themselves generously.

**3. Silence is a tool.** The second half of an answer is usually the true half.
Count to three after she stops.

**4. Write her exact words down, not your summary.** G11 exists to harvest
vocabulary, and a paraphrase destroys the only data it produces. Verbatim or it
did not happen.

---

## A. Must-ask, every call

### A1. How the schedule actually gets made — [G3, G1: H1, H8, H9]

> **"Walk me through how the schedule for next month gets made. Start from the
> very first thing that happens."**

Let her narrate. Only prompt with "and then what?"

Follow-ups, only if not volunteered:

- "Who physically builds it?"
- "What are you looking at while you do that?" (tool, without naming one)
- "Who else touches it before it is posted?"

**What you are listening for:** whether she does it herself or it is delegated
(H8), and what it is built in (H9). At larger hospitals expect a staffing office
(H1), which is the size-transfer question G1 exists to answer.

### A2. The hours — WALK IT, DO NOT ASK IT — [G3: H10]

> **"When was the last time you touched the schedule? What were you doing?"**
> then **"And before that?"** then **"And before that?"**

Keep going back until you have covered a full week. Then, and only then:

> "Roughly how long did each of those take?"

**Do not ask how many hours a week scheduling takes, and never say "8 to 12".**
H10 predicts she will understate it, because callout chasing happens on evenings
and phones and is not filed mentally under "scheduling". If you ask directly you
get the understated number; if you then supply our figure you have led her on
the one number the hero copy rests on.

**The finding is the gap.** After walking it, you may ask "if you had to put a
number on a normal week, what would you say?" The difference between that answer
and the walked total is the most valuable thing this call can produce.

### A3. The last callout — [G1, G2: H2, H5, H6, H7]

> **"Tell me about the last time someone called out for a night shift. What
> happened?"**

Then: **"What went through your head first?"**

**This is the N9 question and it settles the held positioning statement P11, so
do not rush it.** Say nothing after "what went through your head first". Do not
offer "patient safety", do not offer "the nurse". Whichever she names first, and
in whose words, is the finding (H5). If she draws a line from unfair rotation to
tired nurses to mistakes without being asked, that is H6 confirmed, and it only
counts if it was unprompted.

Follow-up: **"Who did you call, and how did you decide who to call first?"**
(H2: how many realistic candidates exist, and whether charge-qualified changes
it.)

### A4. What her CEO asks about — [G10: H18, and we have NO belief here]

> **"When you and your CEO talk, what do they ask you about?"**

Then: **"What would make it a good quarter, from their point of view?"**

**Do not ask "what are you graded on"** — that invites a job description rather
than the truth. This is one of two questions where we recorded no prediction at
all, which makes it the least contaminated and possibly the most valuable thing
on the list. Every "what you promise" statement in `POSITIONING.md` currently
rests on our guess at this answer.

## B. Ask as many as time allows

### B1. What they have already tried — [G4: H11]

> **"What have you tried, or bought, to make scheduling easier?"**

Follow-up: **"How did that go?"** and, if agency comes up, **"How do you think
about what agency costs you?"**

H11 says agency spend is universal and a hired scheduler is the one thing they
never do. Listen for whether she connects agency premium to scheduling at all,
because if she does not, that connection is the sale.

Then, for H3: **"When you use agency, is that a normal week or a bad week?"**
H3 predicts routine use at larger hospitals and last-resort use at 25 beds
because of cost. Since most of these calls are with hospitals larger than our
ICP, her answer is a size-transfer data point rather than a fact about our
buyer, which is exactly what G1 exists to sort.

### B1b. How it differs by size — [G1: H4]

> **"Have you worked somewhere smaller than here? How was scheduling different
> there?"**

If she has not: **"Do you know anyone running a much smaller place? What is it
like for them?"**

H4 is our ICP floor: below roughly 10 nurses the DON runs Excel without enough
pain to pay $1,000 a month. That number defines the bottom of our market and we
have never tested it. Do not ask it as a price question, and do not name a
figure. What you want is where she thinks the problem stops being real, in her
own framing.

### B2. What happens if she decides to buy — [G5: H12, H13]

> **"Say you decided tomorrow that you wanted outside help with this. What
> actually happens next?"**

Follow-ups: **"Whose budget would that come out of?"** and **"Who else has to
say yes?"**

Tests H12 (admin/operations, CEO signs) and H13 (two yeses). If a board,
committee or IT review appears, that is a surprise worth chasing hard.

### B3. When anything last changed — [G6: H14, our other blank]

> **"When was the last time anything changed about how scheduling works here?
> What prompted it?"**

If nothing has changed: **"What would have to happen for it to change?"**

We recorded no belief about what locks scheduling spend. Listen for whether the
blocker is a contract, a budget cycle, or simply that nobody has raised it.

### B4. Taking a vendor seriously — [G7: H15]

> **"You must get pitched fairly often. What makes you actually take one
> seriously?"**

Then the sharper one: **"Has anyone ever earned that with you? What did they
do?"**

H15 says trust comes in two stages: proof on their own roster first, peer word
of mouth after. If she describes wanting to see it work on her real data before
committing, that is the offer design question, and it is the founder's call
rather than something this call settles.

### B5. What would make it urgent — [G8: H16]

> **"What would have to happen for this to become a problem you deal with this
> quarter rather than eventually?"**

H16 predicts nurse retention first, then cost, then audits. Note the order she
gives, not just the contents.

### B6. What would worry her — [G9: H17]

> **"If you did bring something in to help with this, what would worry you
> about it?"**

Ask this BEFORE describing anything we do. H17 predicts losing the final call
and an assumed IT and EMR burden. Both are already untrue of us, so the
temptation to reassure her mid-answer will be strong. Do not. Let her finish;
the objection in her words is the data.

### B7. How she would look for help — [G12: H20]

> **"Walk me through the last time you needed to solve a problem like this and
> did not know who to call. What did you actually do first?"**

**Never ask "where do you find vendors" or "do you use Google".** Self-reported
channel behaviour is unreliable in exactly the direction that would let us keep
believing whatever we already believe. Founder ruling 2026-08-30: content is not
the primary acquisition channel, contacts are, so search appearing here would be
a genuine surprise rather than a confirmation.

## C. Harvest, do not ask — [G11: H7, H19]

There is no question for these. They are collected by writing down what she
says all the way through.

- **What does she call the thing?** "Scheduling software", "a scheduler",
  "staffing", something else (H19 predicts software).
- **What does she call the risk?** H7 predicts concrete incident words —
  "falls", "med errors", "someone codes and we're short" — rather than the
  formal phrase "patient safety". If she says "patient safety" unprompted, that
  is a real finding against us.
- **What does she call the people?** Nurses, staff, the floor, my team.
- **What does she call the pain?** Note the verb she uses for chasing cover.

These words go straight into `positioning-registry.md` vocabulary. They are the
cheapest and most durable output of the whole exercise.

---

## D. Close, every call

> **"This has been really useful. Is there anyone you would suggest I talk to,
> particularly at a critical access hospital in Texas?"**

Standing instruction from `GOALS.md`. H21 says the NAINA route is a personal
network rather than a repeatable channel, and personal networks only extend by
being asked.

---

## After each call, same day

Write up while it is fresh. For each hypothesis touched: **confirmed,
contradicted, or not reached.** A hypothesis nobody addressed is not evidence
for it.

Two disciplines that decide whether this exercise is worth anything:

**Record contradictions in the founder's own words, not softened.** The point of
writing beliefs down beforehand was to make them falsifiable. A hypothesis that
survives because it got quietly reworded has taught us nothing.

**Chase surprises.** Anything that genuinely surprises you is worth more than
any confirmation, and it should change the next call's questions. Stop
interviewing when the surprises stop, not when the list is finished.
