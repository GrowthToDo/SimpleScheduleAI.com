Maps to `GOALS.md` in this directory (the CNO interview chain, prepared for
the NAINA introductions). These are the founder's current, unvalidated
beliefs, recorded before interviewing so that reality can confirm or
contradict each one. They are bets, not facts: nothing here may be quoted in
site copy or content, which remains governed by the facts dossier.

## Hypotheses

**H1.** At hospitals of roughly 100 beds and up, the schedule is built by a
dedicated scheduler or a centralized staffing office, not personally by the
CNO or nurse manager. [G1]

**H2.** When a nurse calls out at a 25-bed hospital, the manager typically
has 3 or fewer realistic replacement candidates, and for a charge-qualified
shift sometimes none. At a large hospital the same callout is absorbed by a
float pool or a deeper roster. [G1]

**H3.** Large hospitals use agency staff routinely to fill coverage gaps; a
25-bed hospital treats agency as a last resort because of cost. [G1, G4]

**H4.** Below roughly 10 nurses on the roster, the DON runs the schedule in
Excel without enough pain to pay $1,000 a month. [G1]

**H5.** Asked about a short shift, a CNO's first unprompted worry is double:
the patient AND the specific nurse covering the hole, especially a nurse who
has covered several recent gaps and is close to burning out. [G2]

**H6.** Experienced nurse leaders describe the causal chain themselves,
unprompted: unfair rotation wears nurses down, and worn-down nurses make
mistakes. [G2]

**H7.** CNOs talk about short-shift risk in concrete incident words ("falls",
"med errors", "someone codes and we're short"), not the formal phrase
"patient safety". [G2, G11]

**H8.** At a 25-bed hospital the DON or nurse manager builds the schedule
personally, rather than delegating it to a charge nurse or an administrative
staffer. [G3]

**H9.** They build it in Excel, not on paper and not in a scheduling module of
a system they already pay for. [G3]

**H10.** Asked directly how long scheduling takes, a CNO will UNDERSTATE it,
naming something like 3 to 5 hours. Our 8 to 12 only appears once the pieces
are added up for her, because callout chasing is scattered across evenings and
weekends and is not filed mentally under "scheduling". [G3]

> **H10 changes the interview question, so it must not be asked directly.**
> "How many hours does scheduling take?" tests nothing: if H10 is right the
> answer is wrong by construction, and if we then supply "8 to 12" we have led
> the witness on the one number our hero copy rests on. Walk the week instead
> — when did you last touch the schedule, what happened, how long did that
> take — and total it afterwards. The gap between her stated figure and the
> walked total IS the finding, and it is more useful than either number alone.

**H11.** A small hospital has spent real money on scheduling pain, but almost
never on a scheduler. Agency premium is certain and universal. A cheap tool
that failed, or a scheduling module bundled inside a system too complex to run
at this size, are each common but not universal. **Hiring a person to do it is
the one option they do not take.** [G4]

> Consequence if H11 holds: our $1,000 to $1,500 a month never competes against
> a salary. It competes against agency premium the hospital does not currently
> attribute to scheduling, which means the sale depends on connecting the two.
> That is a content and discovery job, not a pricing one.

**H12.** The money comes from the administrative or operations budget and the
CEO or administrator signs, not from the nursing department budget the nurse
leader controls. [G5]

**H13.** Two yeses move money: the nurse leader champions it and the CEO or
administrator signs. Not a board, not a committee, and not an IT review. [G5]

> H12 and H13 together are the case for walking the administrator/CEO needs
> ladder in `NEEDS-STACK.md`, which has never been done. If the signer sits
> outside nursing, every statement in `POSITIONING.md` aimed only at the nurse
> leader is aimed at the champion, not the buyer.
