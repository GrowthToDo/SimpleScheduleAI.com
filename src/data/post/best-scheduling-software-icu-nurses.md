The best ICU nurse scheduling software for a small hospital is the one that knows who on your roster can safely work the unit tonight, and who can only work it beside someone more experienced. Most scheduling platforms know license type well. Fewer know that two of your RNs are in their first year on the ICU. Fewer still will stop a draft that puts those two nurses on the same night. This guide compares eight platforms on what their own pages document about ICU staffing, and says plainly where that documentation runs out.

This guide is for the minority of small hospitals that run ICU beds. In the most recent national count, 380 of 1,268 critical access hospitals (30.0%) had an intensive care unit, from 2008 to 2009 data ([Joynt and colleagues, JAMA, 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21730240/)). We found no newer count. For the wider comparison on every unit, see our guide to [nurse scheduling software](/nurse-scheduling-software).

## What Makes ICU Nurse Scheduling Different at a Small Hospital?

The pool is small and the experience mix matters on every shift. A critical access hospital with an ICU often runs only a handful of beds. A [2005 rural health study](https://www.shepscenter.unc.edu/wp-content/uploads/2014/10/WP81.pdf) interviewed 63 critical access hospitals that provided intensive care. Across those 63 hospitals, the average was 3.5 intensive care beds, ranging from 1 to 9.

A unit that small is staffed by a small group of nurses your hospital has signed off for it. Covering an ICU shift is harder than covering a floor shift for three reasons:

- **Unit competency.** A fully licensed RN may not have completed your ICU orientation. Software that only knows license type will offer that nurse for an ICU shift.
- **Experience on the same shift.** Two nurses in their first year on the unit can each be signed off and still be a weak pair for a night. [AACN's 2024 staffing standards](https://www.aacn.org/newsroom/standards-published-for-critical-care-nurse-staffing) say staffing plans and patient assignments "support the unique needs of nurses who are new to the unit."
- **Census swings.** In a unit that small, one admission can change the number of nurses you need. The schedule has to change when census does.

Charge adds a fourth problem. Covering charge is hard enough on its own, which our guide to [charge nurse scheduling software](/blog/best-charge-nurse-scheduling-software) covers in depth.

## Is There a Required ICU Nurse-to-Patient Ratio?

No federal rule sets one. The hospital Conditions of Participation ask for "adequate numbers" of nurses and other staff. That staffing must be enough "to provide nursing care to all patients as needed" ([42 CFR 482.23(b)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-42/chapter-IV/subchapter-G/part-482/subpart-C/section-482.23)). For a critical access hospital, [42 CFR 485.631(a)(5)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-42/chapter-IV/subchapter-G/part-485/subpart-F/section-485.631) requires "a registered nurse, clinical nurse specialist, or licensed practical nurse" on duty whenever there is an inpatient. Neither names a number.

Texas sets no ICU ratio either, as of November 2026. Texas requires every hospital to run a nurse staffing committee, and [Texas Health and Safety Code section 257.004](https://codes.findlaw.com/tx/health-and-safety-code/health-safety-sect-257-004/) says at least 60 percent of its members must be RNs who give direct patient care. The committee recommends a staffing plan. The chapter has no exemption for small or rural hospitals.

A few states do set ICU numbers. California requires one licensed nurse for every two patients in critical care units "at all times" ([22 CCR 70217](https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/california/Cal-Code-Regs-Tit-22-SS-70217)). Massachusetts sets one nurse to one or two ICU patients, depending on how stable each patient is ([M.G.L. c. 111, section 231](https://malegislature.gov/Laws/GeneralLaws/PartI/TitleXVI/Chapter111/Section231)). Our [state-by-state ratio guide](/blog/nurse-to-patient-ratios-by-state) has the rest.

The professional standard is AACN's. Its 2024 standards say "organizational staffing plans anticipate that critically ill or injured patients generally require a ratio of one nurse to two patients." AACN's ratio is a planning expectation, not a legal minimum. A second AACN standard matters for the schedule. It says charge nurses "are not included in patient assignments, except in rare crisis situations." So a unit staffed at one nurse to two patients needs the charge nurse on top of the bedside count. Our guide to [nurse staffing ratios at small hospitals](/blog/nurse-staffing-ratios-critical-access-hospital) covers how to set your own.

Here is how those checks play out on one night. The hospital in this example is made up. Your ICU has four beds and three patients tonight. At one nurse to two patients, three patients need two bedside nurses. One is your night nurse with eight years on the unit. The other finished ICU orientation in the spring.

At 5 p.m. the eight-year nurse calls out. Every nurse who is free and still under 40 hours this week also finished orientation this year. Any one of those nurses fills the empty slot. Both bedside nurses would then have less than a year on the unit. A tool that checks license type sees a covered shift. A tool that checks experience on the shift sees that nobody experienced is left, which is what you need to know at 5 p.m.

## 1. SimpleScheduleAI

<img src="/images/blog/best-scheduling-software-icu-nurses/SimpleScheduleAI.webp" alt="SimpleScheduleAI nurse scheduling software interface" width="1200" height="630" loading="lazy" class="rounded-lg border border-slate-200 my-4" />

SimpleScheduleAI is AI-native [nurse scheduling software](/nurse-scheduling-software), delivered as a service: the AI builds the schedule, a scheduling team checks it, the nurse manager approves. It is built for Texas hospitals of 25 beds or fewer, and [how it works](/how-it-works) is set out step by step. It is new and has no public reviews yet.

**Best for:** Small hospitals with an ICU, where a handful of experienced nurses cover every night between them.

**Key advantages:**

- Every nurse on your roster carries a competency level from 1 to 5, set by your hospital. Level 1 is an orientee who needs a preceptor. Level 5 is an expert who can precept and take charge. The levels use the names of [Patricia Benner's novice-to-expert stages](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/6917683/).
- Three of the 13 safety rules shape every ICU shift before a draft reaches you. Every ICU shift gets a Level 4 or 5 nurse, placed before the other slots are filled. If none is available, the draft says so instead of filling the shift without one. A Level 1 nurse is never placed on an ICU shift, and a Level 1 nurse on any unit always works beside a Level 5 preceptor.
- The same rules cover any unit that has ICU, ER, ED or Emergency as a word in its name, so your emergency department gets the same checks.
- A charge slot goes only to a nurse your roster lists as charge-qualified and at Level 4 or above. ICU charge shifts are filled first, weekend charge before weekday charge. Filling them last would leave the charge nurses with most of their weekly hours already used.
- Census ranges, set with you at onboarding, say how many RNs the unit needs at each patient count, such as two RNs for three or four patients. Each shift also carries a census level, from Blue to Red, and a busier level raises the number of nurses the draft must place.
- When a nurse calls out, you get a ranked shortlist. Nurses who would stay under 40 hours this week, and so avoid overtime, come first, and each name shows its competency level against the nurse who called out. If the nurse who called out held charge, anyone not charge-qualified is marked ineligible, and if nobody qualifies the shortlist says so.

**Key limitations:**

- The callout shortlist does not drop a nurse whose level is too low, and it does not check whether the shift still has a Level 4 or 5 nurse after the callout. It shows each nurse's level next to the level of the nurse who called out. In the 5 p.m. example above, every free nurse would show as below the nurse who called out, and the choice stays with you.
- The rules follow the unit's name. A unit called CCU or Special Care is not covered unless its name includes ICU.
- Each schedule is built ahead of time from the census level on each shift. It does not read a live census from your EHR.
- Competency levels are your hospital's judgment. No rule reads certifications such as CCRN or ACLS, and nothing tracks expiry dates. Keep those in your credentialing system.
- It staffs shifts. Which nurse takes which two patients stays with your charge nurse.
- The service covers Texas only, and it does not schedule physicians or intensivists.

**Verdict:** The strongest fit if your ICU risk is a night with nobody experienced on, and nobody at your hospital has time to maintain scheduling rules. Look elsewhere if you need physician scheduling in the same system or you are outside Texas. Our [AI nurse scheduling](/ai-nurse-scheduling) overview explains how each draft is built.

**Cost:** $1,000 a month for up to 20 nurses, $1,500 for 21 to 40. One price per hospital, not per nurse.

## 2. NurseGrid Manager

<img src="/images/blog/best-scheduling-software-icu-nurses/Nursegrid.webp" alt="NurseGrid Manager scheduling interface" width="1200" height="568" loading="lazy" class="rounded-lg border border-slate-200 my-4" />

NurseGrid Manager is the manager tier of the NurseGrid app, owned by HealthStream. Our [NurseGrid Manager explainer](/blog/what-is-nursegrid-manager) covers how it works. It holds 3.9/5 on Capterra (13 reviews; small sample, checked November 2026). It is the only platform here whose marketing names critical access hospitals: "Usually teams with 250 staff or fewer. Think critical access hospitals, hospital departments, surgery centers, clinics, and more."

**Best for:** Small hospitals that want to run the schedule themselves at a published price, with coverage set by position.

**Key advantages:**

- Coverage requirements are set per position. An unmet one shows before you publish: the cell turns red, with a folded corner in the lower right.
- The requirement also gates self-scheduling. If a Day shift needs an RN, LPNs and CNAs cannot sign up for it.
- Shifts can be marked as charge or preceptor shifts, and staff can be grouped by "charge trained status" when you message them.
- Pricing is public: "$5 per month, per staff member. Got 25 staff? That's $125/month."

**Key limitations:**

- Requirements work by position, not by unit competency. The words skill, competency and certification appear in none of its 30 help topics, checked November 2026.
- Nothing in its documentation restricts a charge shift to charge-trained staff.
- Its own help center says coverage requirements "do not automatically create or approve open shift requests." A gap shows in the cell, and filling it is up to you.
- Census and acuity do not appear in its help center.
- An assistant director of nursing reported that only the schedule's manager can edit it:

  > "unable to adjust schedule unless you are the manager of the schedule. this is not helpful when other employees have to take call and manage the schedule."
  >
  > Reviewer, ADON, June 12, 2024, Capterra

- Reviewers report that the manager phone app was removed in 2024.

  > "It no longer has the manager app so I have to login to desktop to make changes."
  >
  > Reviewer, Chief Nursing Officer, June 18, 2024, Capterra

**Verdict:** The closest self-run option for a small hospital, and the cheapest published price here. It fits if position-level coverage is enough for your ICU, desktop-only manager access is acceptable, and you will check experience on each night yourself.

**Cost:** $5 per staff member per month.

## 4. In-House Health

<img src="/images/blog/best-scheduling-software-icu-nurses/InHouseHealth.webp" alt="In-House Health scheduling platform interface" width="1200" height="563" loading="lazy" class="rounded-lg border border-slate-200 my-4" />

In-House Health sells AI scheduling to hospitals and skilled nursing facilities. Of the seven other platforms here, it is the only one whose product page we found naming the ICU: "In-House can give recommendations even for high acuity units like ICUs."

In-House Health has no Capterra listing, and its G2 listing has no reviews yet, so we have no reviewer quote to share.

**Best for:** Hospitals that want staffing forecasts driven by how sick patients are and how busy each shift will be.

**Key advantages:**

- Forecasting covers census and acuity: "Predictive scheduling uses AI to forecast patient census, acuity levels and staffing needs in advance."
- Each shift is weighed by workload, "factoring in patient acuity, volume of admits/discharges."

## What to Do This Week

1. Write down every nurse signed off for your ICU, with the month each one finished orientation. If that list lives only in your head, it is not yet a list a scheduling tool can use.
2. Mark your experienced nurses, the ones you would trust to be the most senior nurse on a night. Then count, across last month's schedule, the ICU nights with none of those experienced nurses on.
3. Take one recent ICU callout and write down how you filled it. Note whether the replacement was new to the unit, and whether you had a better name you did not reach in time. Our guide to [handling nurse callouts](/blog/how-to-handle-nurse-callouts) has a fuller method.
4. Check your staffing plan against AACN's expectation of one nurse to two critically ill patients, and decide whether your charge nurse sits inside or outside that count. Our guide to [acuity-based staffing](/blog/acuity-based-staffing-small-hospital) shows how to adjust the count for sicker patients.
5. On every vendor demo, ask the 5 p.m. question: my most experienced ICU nurse just called out for tonight, and every nurse who is free is new to the unit. What does your product show me? Watch the screen, not the slides. If the honest answer at your hospital is that you check experience on each night by memory, that is the gap the SimpleScheduleAI [critical access hospital scheduling](/critical-access-hospital-scheduling) service is built to close.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: What is the required nurse-to-patient ratio in an ICU?**
No federal rule sets one, and Texas sets none, as of November 2026. California requires one nurse for every two critical care patients at all times, and Massachusetts sets one or two ICU patients per nurse depending on how stable each patient is. AACN's 2024 standards say staffing plans should anticipate that critically ill patients generally need one nurse for every two patients.

**Q: Does ICU scheduling software check CCRN certification?**
Some platforms say they factor in licensure, and UKG says an add-on enforces licensure requirements. SimpleScheduleAI does not read certifications such as CCRN in any rule. It uses the competency level your hospital gives each nurse. AACN describes CCRN as a specialty certification, and no federal Condition of Participation requires it to work in an ICU.

**Q: Can scheduling software stop two new nurses from working the same ICU night?**
SimpleScheduleAI does by rule. Every ICU shift gets a Level 4 or 5 nurse, and if none is available the draft says so. A Level 1 nurse is never placed there. None of the other seven platforms here documents a pairing rule like that on the pages we read. Ask each one to show you on a demo.

**Q: What is the cheapest ICU nurse scheduling software for a small hospital?**
NurseGrid Manager publishes the lowest price here, $5 per staff member per month, which is $125 a month for 25 staff. SimpleScheduleAI is $1,000 a month for up to 20 nurses, with the schedule built and checked for you. The other six do not publish prices.
