## Key Takeaways

- **Count the need before you build.** Count the people each shift needs by role: licensed nurses, plus aides if you use them. Turn that into weekly hours and divide by the hours one person works a week (36 on three 12-hour shifts, 40 on five 8-hour shifts). Then add people to cover time off.
- **Write down every rule and who set it.** At a critical access hospital, [42 CFR 485.631(a)(5)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-42/chapter-IV/subchapter-G/part-485/subpart-F/section-485.631) requires a licensed nurse on duty whenever there is an inpatient. A charge nurse on every shift is your hospital's own policy.
- **Build the hardest shifts first.** Charge, weekend and night shifts go on the grid before full-time patterns, part-time nurses and PRN (as-needed) nurses.
- **Check every nurse's weekly hours before you post.** Under the [FLSA](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/whd/fact-sheets/54-healthcare-overtime), hospital overtime is generally due after 40 hours in a workweek. A nurse on five 8-hour shifts is already at 40, and a nurse on three 12-hour shifts goes over with one more shift.
- **Cover callouts with volunteers.** In Texas, [Health and Safety Code Chapter 258](https://law.justia.com/codes/texas/health-and-safety-code/title-4/subtitle-b/chapter-258/) bars a hospital from requiring a nurse to work extra hours or extra days beyond their schedule.

Nursing staff scheduling is the work of putting names on shifts for the whole nursing staff. That means registered nurses (RNs) and licensed vocational nurses (LVNs, the Texas title for licensed practical nurses). It also means nurse aides or nurse techs, if your hospital uses them. The schedule decides who works days and nights, who covers each weekend and holiday, and who is charge nurse.

At a small hospital, such as a [critical access hospital](/critical-access-hospital-scheduling) of 25 beds or fewer, every one of those choices touches a short roster. The licensed nurses carry the federal and Texas on-duty rules, so they are the tightest part of the count.

This guide follows the order you would work in: count, write the rules, build, check, post, cover callouts and review. The worked numbers are examples for both 12-hour and 8-hour shifts, so put your own in their place.

## What Does Nursing Staff Scheduling Involve at a Small Hospital?

Nursing staff scheduling at a small hospital covers five jobs. You collect availability and time-off requests, build the schedule, post it, adjust it as things change, and find cover when a nurse calls out. The build happens once per cycle. The other four come back every week, and covering callouts can come up any day.

From our interviews with more than 30 nurse managers, we estimate that scheduling takes a nurse manager 8 to 12 hours a week. About 4 to 6 of those hours go to finding cover for callouts, and building the schedule takes 4 to 6 hours once per four-week cycle (1 to 1.5 hours a week). Our [nurse manager time breakdown](/blog/nurse-manager-scheduling-time-breakdown) shows all five jobs.

A short roster leaves little room for error, because only a few people can cover when one nurse is out. A gap you find in the count is easier to fix than one you find on a Saturday night.

## How Many Nurses Does a Small Hospital Need to Cover Every Shift?

Count the people each shift needs by role: licensed nurses (RNs and LVNs) and any aides or nurse techs. Turn those shifts into weekly hours, divide by the hours one person works a week, and add people for time off. In one example, two licensed nurses and one aide on every shift takes about 14 to 16 people.

The hours one person works a week depend on your [shift length](/blog/8-hour-vs-12-hour-nursing-shifts). A nurse on three 12-hour shifts works 36 hours, which is 0.9 of a 40-hour full-time week. A nurse on five 8-hour shifts works 40 hours, a full 1.0. A full-time year is 2,080 paid hours, which is 40 hours × 52 weeks. If each full-time equivalent (FTE, one full-time position) takes 250 hours of time off, as in this example, it covers about 1,830 working hours.

Here is the example worked through for both shift lengths. It is an illustration, not a rule or a benchmark, so swap in your own plan, shift length and time off.

Both shift lengths cover the same 336 licensed-nurse hours a week. The 12-hour version needs more people because each nurse works 36 hours instead of 40.

The time-off rows come from our [nurse staffing relief factor guide](/blog/nurse-staffing-relief-factor). Two nurses around the clock is 2 × 24 × 365, or 17,520 hours a year. One aide around the clock is 1 × 24 × 365, or 8,760 hours a year. The 250 hours of time off is that guide's example. For your own number, pull last year's total paid time off hours from your payroll or timekeeping report and divide by your number of FTEs, to get the average per full-time position.

The charge row is the easiest to miss. If one nurse on every shift must be charge-qualified, you need at least 5 of them on either shift length, before anyone takes a vacation. Our guide to [what a charge nurse is](/blog/what-is-a-charge-nurse) explains why that group is small.

Aides do not count toward the licensed-nurse rules in the next section, so count them on their own line. The two-nurse, one-aide grid is our example, not a legal minimum. Our guide to [safe staffing and nurse-to-patient ratios](/blog/nurse-staffing-ratios-critical-access-hospital) covers how a small hospital sets its own numbers. Which days each nurse works is a separate question, covered in our [12-hour nurse schedule examples](/blog/12-hour-hospital-shifts-scheduling).

The federal rule is the [CAH staffing rule at 42 CFR 485.631](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-42/chapter-IV/subchapter-G/part-485/subpart-F/section-485.631), paragraph (a)(5). It says "a registered nurse, clinical nurse specialist, or licensed practical nurse is on duty whenever the CAH has one or more inpatients." Texas licensing goes further. The [Texas hospital licensing rule at 26 TAC 505.41](https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/texas/26-Tex-Admin-Code-SS-505-41) says "An RN shall be on duty in each building of a licensed hospital that contains at least one nursing unit where patients are present." That is paragraph (o)(2)(D). How that applies depends on your license and building layout, so confirm it with your compliance lead or read the rule text linked in Sources.

Texas also wants a written plan. [Health and Safety Code 257.003](https://codes.findlaw.com/tx/health-and-safety-code/health-safety-sect-257-003) requires a nurse staffing policy to "ensure that an adequate number and skill mix of nurses are available to meet the level of patient care needed."

Then write one line per nurse. Note employment type: full-time, part-time or PRN, meaning a per diem nurse who works as needed. Add the days they cannot work, whether they are charge-qualified, the units they are cleared for, and any certification a unit requires.

## How Do You Create a Nurse Schedule Step by Step?

Build in a fixed order, hardest shifts first. Enter time off and the dates PRN nurses offered. Fill charge shifts, then weekend and night shifts. Lay down full-time patterns, then fill the rest with part-time and PRN nurses. Check the whole grid, and mark anything unfilled, before you post.

Charge shifts go first because they draw on the smallest group. Fill weekend charge shifts early. If you leave them to the end, your charge nurses may already be at their hours for the week.

## How Far in Advance Should You Post a Nurse Schedule?

We suggest posting at least two weeks before the cycle starts. That gives nurses time to arrange childcare, school and second jobs, and gives you time to fill the shifts you marked as unfilled. Your hospital's own policy or contract may set a longer lead time.

Keep the dates the same every cycle. Set a deadline for time-off requests. Set the week you build the schedule. Set the date you post it. Write all three into your scheduling policy so nurses know when the next schedule arrives.

A fixed calendar matters as much as the lead time. Requests that arrive late mean rebuilding the schedule. A change made late can lead a nurse to call out to keep a plan they already made.

## How Do You Keep a Nurse Schedule Fair for Weekends and Holidays?

Set a number for weekends, count weekends and holidays for every nurse, and carry the counts from one cycle to the next. "No more than two weekends in a four-week cycle" is one example threshold. Pick yours, write it down, and apply it when you build.

Without a number, weekend shifts go to whoever agrees first. Over a few months, the same few nurses can end up carrying most of them.

Keep holiday records for the whole year, so next year starts from who worked each holiday. Our [fair holiday rotation guide](/blog/fair-holiday-rotation-nurses-cah) sets out a method that rotates holidays across three groups of nurses.

If your nurses rotate between days and nights, count nights per nurse too. A nurse who worked extra nights this cycle should work fewer next cycle, and the same goes for weekends. Share the counts so each nurse can see where they stand.

## How Do You Check a Nurse Schedule for Overtime and Fatigue Before Posting?

Total each nurse's hours for every workweek before you post. Under the FLSA, hospital overtime is generally due after 40 hours in a workweek. Then check shifts in a row against your own cap, because federal law sets none.

[DOL Fact Sheet #54](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/whd/fact-sheets/54-healthcare-overtime) covers overtime for hospitals. Texas adds no state overtime layer on top of the federal rule. Instead of counting 40 hours in a workweek, a hospital may adopt the optional 8-and-80 system. It counts overtime past 8 hours in a day or 80 hours in 14 days. Our guide on [how to reduce nurse overtime](/blog/how-to-reduce-nurse-overtime) explains when the 8-and-80 system costs more than the 40-hour count.

A nurse on three 12-hour shifts works 36 hours, so a fourth shift that week makes 48 hours, or 8 hours of overtime. A nurse on five 8-hour shifts is already at 40, so any extra shift that week is overtime. Count every hour the nurse worked for your hospital that week, not only the hours on your unit.

Keep room on your call list for one extra shift. For a 12-hour open shift, look for part-time and PRN nurses at 28 hours or fewer that week, since 28 + 12 = 40. For an 8-hour open shift, look for nurses at 32 hours or fewer, since 32 + 8 = 40.

Fatigue has no federal limit, so the cap on shifts in a row is your policy. For 12-hour shifts, our [12-hour scheduling guide](/blog/12-hour-hospital-shifts-scheduling) recommends no more than 3 consecutive shifts before a day off. For 8-hour shifts, set your own cap the same way and write it down. The [Department of Labor](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/whd/overtime) says this about the Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA): "There is no limit in the Act on the number of hours employees aged 16 and older may work in any workweek."

## How Do You Fix the Nurse Schedule When a Nurse Calls Out?

Work from a ranked call list made before the callout. Put qualified nurses who are not working that day at the top, sorted by hours this week, fewest first. Call in order and log each call. For a routine callout, in Texas, cover has to come from nurses who agree to work.

[Texas Health and Safety Code Chapter 258](https://law.justia.com/codes/texas/health-and-safety-code/title-4/subtitle-b/chapter-258/) bars a hospital from requiring a nurse to work extra hours or extra days beyond their schedule, and a nurse may refuse. The exceptions are narrow, such as a declared emergency or a procedure in progress. Under the exception for an unforeseeable emergency, the hospital must still make a good-faith effort, as far as possible, to cover with volunteers.

So the call list needs enough names: PRN nurses who have offered dates, and part-time nurses with room under 40 hours. Our guide to [building a PRN pool](/blog/prn-pool-critical-access-hospital) covers building that list of PRN nurses. The [callout handling guide](/blog/how-to-handle-nurse-callouts) walks through the list and the log, and [mandatory nurse overtime by state](/blog/mandatory-nurse-overtime-by-state) covers other states.

Once a nurse accepts, update the posted schedule and their weekly hours straight away. Check that the extra shift does not break your cap on shifts in a row.

## How Do You Review a Nurse Schedule at the End of Each Cycle?

At the end of each cycle, look at four things. Which shifts went short, and who covered each callout? How many overtime hours did each nurse work, and were they scheduled or from callouts? How do weekend and holiday counts stand? What changes for the next cycle?

The overtime split tells you where to act. Overtime on the posted schedule is a build problem. Overtime from callouts is a cover problem, usually a thin call list. If the same two nurses covered most gaps, give them fewer weekends next cycle.

Update the roster too: new hires once orientation is signed off, changed availability, and nurses newly signed off for charge. If your charge count keeps coming up short, cross-training one more nurse may help more than a new hire. Our guide to a [nurse schedule audit trail](/blog/nurse-schedule-audit-trail) covers what record to keep.

## What to Do This Week

1. **Monday: count your need.** For each role, count the shifts your plan needs in a week and multiply by the shift length. Divide by the hours one person works a week: 36 on three 12-hour shifts, 40 on five 8-hour shifts. Add people for time off, as in the table above. Compare that figure with your roster, counted as people. In the example it is about 16 people on 12-hour shifts, or about 14.4 on 8-hour shifts.
2. **Tuesday: count your charge-qualified nurses.** Divide your charge shifts per week by the shifts one nurse works a week. In the example that is 14 ÷ 3 on 12-hour shifts, or 21 ÷ 5 on 8-hour shifts, so at least 5 either way. If your charge-qualified nurses are at or near that count, pick one nurse to cross-train.
3. **Wednesday: write the rules page.** List each rule with who set it: federal, Texas or your hospital. Add one line per nurse.
4. **Thursday: pull last cycle's counts.** Weekends, holidays and overtime hours for each nurse. Note anyone far above the rest.
5. **Friday: fix the calendar.** For the next three cycles, set the time-off request deadline, the week you build the schedule, and the posting date. Then tell your nurses.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: How many nurses does a 25-bed hospital need?**

It depends on your staffing plan and shift length, so there is no fixed number. Count the shifts each role needs each week, multiply by the shift length, and divide by the hours one person works a week. In our example, two licensed nurses on every shift takes about 10.6 nurses on 12-hour shifts, or about 9.6 on 8-hour shifts, once time off is counted. One aide on every shift adds about 5.3 or 4.8 people.

**Q: Does CMS require a charge nurse on every shift?**

A charge nurse on every shift is a hospital policy. For a critical access hospital, 42 CFR 485.631(a)(5) requires a registered nurse, clinical nurse specialist or licensed practical nurse on duty whenever there is an inpatient. Texas licensing adds an RN on duty in each building with a nursing unit where patients are present.

**Q: Can a Texas hospital require a nurse to stay and cover a callout?**

Generally no. Texas Health and Safety Code Chapter 258 bars a hospital from requiring a nurse to work extra hours or extra days beyond their schedule, and a nurse may refuse. The exceptions are narrow, such as a declared emergency. A routine callout is covered from a ranked list of nurses who agree to work.

**Q: How long does it take to build a nurse schedule by hand?**

We estimate 4 to 6 hours once per four-week cycle, about 1 to 1.5 hours a week. Finding cover for callouts takes more of the week. Our [nurse manager time breakdown](/blog/nurse-manager-scheduling-time-breakdown) shows the full split.
