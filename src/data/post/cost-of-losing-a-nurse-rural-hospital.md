It takes about [78 days to recruit an experienced RN](https://www.nsinursingsolutions.com/documents/library/nsi_national_health_care_retention_report.pdf), and a Critical Access Hospital has no float pool to cover that gap, so the open shifts fall on overtime, agency rates, and the nurses still on the roster.
- Texas Critical Access Hospitals carry the highest RN turnover of the state's three hospital groups, about [18.2 percent](/rural-hospital-nurse-staffing-statistics), so this recurs often enough to plan around as an annual line item.

The cost of nurse turnover usually gets quoted as one national figure, somewhere around sixty thousand dollars per departure. That number is real, but it hides what a rural hospital actually lives through when one nurse leaves. At a 25-bed hospital, one RN is a far larger share of the nursing staff than at a 300-bed system, and there is no float pool to absorb the gap.

The math below builds the cost of losing one nurse from the sourced national figure, then layers on the parts a small rural hospital feels most. Every number is either linked to a primary source or labeled as an illustrative estimate at a stated rate.

## Why Does Losing One Nurse Cost a Rural Hospital More Than a Large System?

Losing one nurse costs a rural hospital more because a single RN is a much larger share of a small staff, and the facility has none of the buffers a large system uses to absorb a departure. At a roughly 25-nurse hospital, one resignation removes about 4 percent of the nursing workforce at once. At a 300-bed system, the same one nurse is well under one percent.

The proportional hit is only half of it. A large hospital has a float pool, a staffing office, and a recruiting pipeline that keeps candidates warm. A Critical Access Hospital serving a low-volume rural area has thin staffing margins by design, which the [Flex Monitoring Team](https://www.flexmonitoring.org/) documents across the CAH program. When one nurse leaves, the replacement pool shrinks to a short list of individual people, and the open shifts get covered by the same nurses who are already on the schedule.

That pressure compounds where the finances are already tight, and Texas has more rural hospitals vulnerable to closure than any other state, as we break down in [the 2026 rural hospital loss data](/articles/rural-hospital-losses-nurse-staffing-cost). This is why turnover reads differently on a rural roster. Texas has [93 critical access hospitals](https://www.ruralhealthinfo.org/states/texas), and its CAHs carry the highest RN turnover of the state's three hospital groups at about [18.2 percent](/rural-hospital-nurse-staffing-statistics), drawn from the Texas DSHS 2024 Hospital Nurse Staffing Study. One departure a year on a small unit is the baseline to plan around.

## What Is the Cost of Nurse Turnover for a Single RN?

The average cost to turn over one bedside RN is [$60,090](https://www.nsinursingsolutions.com/documents/library/nsi_national_health_care_retention_report.pdf), per the 2026 NSI National Health Care Retention & RN Staffing Report, with average hospital RN turnover at 17.6 percent. That figure bundles separation, the cost of covering the vacancy, orientation, and onboarding for the replacement. It is a national average across 527 hospitals, so treat it as the anchor.

For a rural hospital, the anchor is only where the accounting starts. The parts a small facility feels most sharply sit around that number: the coverage gap while the role is open, the manager time spent rebuilding the schedule by hand, and the fairness cost as the open shifts pile onto the remaining staff. ## What Does the Open Role Cost While You Recruit?

The open role costs a rural hospital in overtime and agency premiums for as long as it takes to hire, and that window is not short. It takes about [78 days to recruit an experienced RN](https://www.nsinursingsolutions.com/documents/library/nsi_national_health_care_retention_report.pdf), roughly 11 weeks. With no float pool, every shift on that vacant line has to be covered by someone at a higher rate or by the staff already on the roster.

Here is the math, with the assumptions stated. A full-time RN line runs about 36 hours a week, three 12-hour shifts. Over the roughly 11-week vacancy, that is about 400 shift-hours to backfill. At a $50 per hour loaded rate, covering those hours at straight time would run about $20,000. Covered on overtime or agency at a $75 per hour premium rate, it runs closer to $30,000. The premium, the extra cost of covering the gap instead of having a nurse in the seat, is about $10,000. This is an illustrative figure at the stated rates only.

The premium is only the cash part. The same coverage gap concentrates overtime on a handful of nurses, which is the after-hours pattern we break down in our guide to [after-hours callout coverage at small hospitals](/blog/after-hours-callout-coverage-small-hospitals). Each of those covered shifts is also a manager building a call list by hand, at a facility with no staffing office to hand it to.

## How Does Fair Scheduling Connect to Nurse Retention?

Fair, predictable scheduling connects to retention because unfair schedules are one of the few turnover drivers a nurse manager can act on directly. The [American Nurses Association](https://www.nursingworld.org/practice-policy/nurse-staffing/) treats adequate, well-distributed staffing as central to keeping nurses, and frames it as one of several strategies that work together rather than a single fix. Scheduling is the lever a small hospital owns without new budget.

The mechanism is not complicated. When the same reliable nurses absorb the open shifts, the nights, and the holidays cycle after cycle, they either get used to it or they leave. At a 25-bed hospital, two or three over-burdened nurses are a resignation waiting to happen, and losing one of them restarts the $60,090 clock. We trace how that load concentrates on a few willing nurses in [nurse burnout and the CAH schedule](/blog/nurse-burnout-scheduling-cah).

To be clear about the limits of this claim: no scheduling method reduces turnover by a fixed percentage, and anyone who quotes you one is guessing. What fair scheduling does is remove a known, controllable irritant. Distributing weekend and holiday load evenly before individual preferences are applied is a retention practice with no guarantee attached, and it is worth doing because it is one of the cheapest retention moves available to a rural hospital.

## What to Do This Week

1. **Put a real number on your last departure.** Start with the $60,090 replacement anchor as your floor, then look at your own overtime and agency spend for the weeks the line sat open, remembering the NSI figure already absorbs part of this cost. 3. **Set a fairness floor before the next cycle.** Cap undesirable-shift load per nurse per cycle, and distribute weekends and holidays before anyone self-picks. This is a policy change you can make without software.
