## Key Takeaways

- Mandatory nurse overtime is limited by state law rather than federal law. We read the statute or rule in seven states, and all seven bar a hospital from requiring hours beyond the shift a nurse agreed to work.
- Texas is one of them. [Texas Health and Safety Code Chapter 258](https://law.justia.com/codes/texas/health-and-safety-code/title-4/subtitle-b/chapter-258/) prohibits a hospital from requiring a nurse to work hours beyond her scheduled shifts, and a nurse may refuse mandatory overtime.
- Refusing is protected, but not everywhere that restricts the overtime itself. Texas, Pennsylvania and Washington all bar retaliation against a nurse who declines. The New York statute restricts the overtime without giving the nurse that separate protection.
- The exceptions are narrower than they sound. One of the four Texas exceptions is an emergency the hospital could not have seen coming, and a hospital may only use it after trying voluntary overtime, agency nurses and off-duty nurses. California and Massachusetts carry their own version of that voluntary-first condition.
- Neither a single unplanned callout nor chronic short staffing is an emergency, which together covers most of the nights a nurse gets asked to stay. [Massachusetts writes the second half into the statute](https://malegislature.gov/Laws/GeneralLaws/PartI/TitleXVI/Chapter111/Section226): mandatory overtime "shall not be used as a practice for providing appropriate staffing for the level of patient care required."
- Published counts of the overtime states disagree. The [American Federation of Teachers](https://www.aft.org/healthcare/our-positions/mandatory-overtime) names 16, other lists name 18, and most carry no citation to check. Treat any count as a starting point, then read your own state's text.

## Can a Nurse Refuse Mandatory Overtime?

In some states, yes, and Texas is one of them. In those states a hospital may not require a nurse to work past the shift she agreed to, outside a narrow set of emergencies. Texas, and some but not all of the others, also bar the hospital from punishing her for saying no. That second protection does not come automatically with the first. Everywhere else there is no state rule, and refusal is a matter between the nurse and her employer.

The right has limits. It applies to hours beyond the schedule, not to the schedule itself. A hospital can still build a nurse a 12-hour shift, still ask her to pick up an extra one, and still run prescheduled on-call. What it cannot do is order her to stay when the shift ends because nobody came in to relieve her.

## What Counts as Mandatory Overtime?

It is overtime a nurse is required to work, past the hours she was scheduled for. The trigger is the requirement, not the hour count. On an 8-hour shift, a 9th hour she is ordered to work counts. On a 12-hour shift, a 13th hour she volunteers for does not.

The phrase the statutes use is "hours she agreed to work," and that wording confuses people who have never signed anything with a nurse. It means the shift you posted and she accepted by working it. A posted schedule is the agreement. Self-scheduling does not change that, because she still picked shifts and you still published them.

Two things get mistaken for mandatory overtime. Prescheduled on-call time is excluded in Texas and New York, so a call-in from an on-call rotation does not count. Brief handoff at the end of a shift is also excluded. The New York [Department of Labor](https://dol.ny.gov/mandatory-overtime-nurses) treats shift report as regularly scheduled hours, and bars an employer from using on-call time as a substitute.

Keep all of this separate from overtime pay. These laws govern assignment, meaning whether you can make somebody stay. Pay is a separate question, governed by the federal Fair Labor Standards Act, and it is answered the same way in every state. Our guide to [nursing overtime rules in Texas](/blog/texas-nursing-overtime-compliance-cah) covers the pay side.

Three of the seven go further and protect the nurse who says no. Texas, [Pennsylvania](https://codes.findlaw.com/pa/title-43-ps-labor/pa-st-sect-43-932-3/) and [Washington](https://app.leg.wa.gov/RCW/default.aspx?cite=49.28.140) bar an employer from disciplining, penalizing or otherwise acting against her for refusing. Washington is the most explicit about how small that action can be, naming a threat of discipline alongside dismissal.

Do not assume the protection travels with the prohibition. [New York](https://dol.ny.gov/mandatory-overtime-nurses) restricts the overtime and fines the employer for requiring it, and its statute gives the nurse no separate anti-retaliation right. A state can bar the practice without arming the person it protects.

Eleven more states appear on the published lists: Alaska, Connecticut, Illinois, Maine, Maryland, Minnesota, Missouri, New Hampshire, New Jersey, Rhode Island and West Virginia. They are named here so you can tell whether yours is in play at all, and no further, because we have not read those statutes and will not summarize a law we have not opened. If you operate in one of them, ask your state hospital association for the citation before relying on a blog table, this one included.

How many patients a nurse may be assigned is a separate state map with a different set of states on it, covered in [nurse-to-patient ratios by state](/blog/nurse-to-patient-ratios-by-state). That map has its own count of 16, from a different organization and covering staffing laws generally. The two numbers are not measuring the same thing, so do not carry one across to the other.

Nothing federal applies today. A bill that would extend limits to Medicare-participating facilities nationwide is [backed by the American Nurses Association](https://www.nursingworld.org/news/news-releases/2026-news-releases/american-nurses-association-backs-federal-bill-to-curb-mandatory-overtime-and-protect-patient-safety/) and sitting in committee as of September 2026, so it changes nothing for now.

## What Do the Emergency Exceptions Actually Allow?

Less than most people assume. Every law we read carves out genuine emergencies, and every one of them puts a condition on the carve-out. The condition is the part that gets skipped.

Texas allows four, and only four:

1. A health care disaster in your county or a neighboring one.
2. A declared federal, state or county emergency.
3. An emergency the hospital could not reasonably have seen coming.
4. A procedure already in progress that the nurse cannot safely leave.

Number three is the one hospitals reach for, and it carries a condition. Before using it, the hospital has to try voluntary overtime, agency nurses and off-duty nurses first.

California puts the same idea in a different form. A chief nursing officer or authorized executive has to declare the emergency, and the employer has to show that reasonable steps were taken to provide the required staffing and that continued overtime is still necessary. Massachusetts asks for a good faith effort at voluntary coverage first, and then adds the sentence that decides most real cases:

> "Mandatory overtime shall not be used as a practice for providing appropriate staffing for the level of patient care required."

Read together, these say something a nurse manager already knows. A snowstorm and a bus rollover are emergencies. A unit that runs short every third Tuesday because the roster was never built to cover it is a staffing plan, and no exception covers a staffing plan.

## Does an Unplanned Callout Count as an Emergency Exception?

Generally no, and this is the case that matters, because it is most of them. A nurse calling in sick is not a disaster and not a declared emergency. It is also not unforeseeable in the sense the statutes mean, because callouts are a known feature of running a unit. Our [callout handling guide](/blog/how-to-handle-nurse-callouts) takes the same position.

So the honest version of Tuesday night is uncomfortable. Voluntary coverage genuinely failed, it is not an emergency, and in Texas you cannot order her to stay. The statute closes that door and does not open another one, which means the remaining moves are operational rather than legal:

- Work the voluntary list further out. Off-duty nurses who were not called, the PRN pool, agency if you hold agreements.
- Put a qualified licensed body on the floor from somewhere else, including the DON or the nurse manager.
- Change what the unit is carrying. Hold admissions, divert, or move a patient.

The first two are yours. The third is a nursing leadership and administrator call, and it is the one worth having decided in advance rather than at 7 p.m. A hospital that has never discussed which of these it would choose will reach for the order it is not allowed to give.

## Can a Texas Hospital Require a Nurse to Stay Late?

Only inside those four exceptions, and only after trying to fill the hours another way. Two details decide who this covers. Chapter 258 protects registered nurses and licensed vocational nurses, and Texas issues the LVN license that most other states call an LPN, so those are one job under two names. Aides and techs are not covered at all.

Retaliation is handled separately. A hospital may not take action against a nurse for refusing hours the statute protects. That protection matters more than the prohibition itself, because the pressure at 7 p.m. is rarely a formal order. It is a question asked in a tone that makes refusing feel expensive.

**Who would ever come asking?** The chapter carries its own enforcement and complaint-resolution section, and the anti-retaliation protection covers a nurse who reports a violation as well as one who refuses to stay. The likeliest route is a complaint from the nurse you asked. On whether Texas makes the hospital report its own use of an exception the way Massachusetts does, the sections we read do not create that duty. Treat that as an absence rather than an exemption, and ask your counsel.

One clarification is worth making inside your own building. Texas sets no state overtime pay rule. People hear that and conclude "Texas does not regulate nurse overtime." On assignment, Texas is one of the stricter states, so that conclusion is wrong in the direction that gets a hospital in trouble.

## What Does a Refusal Right Mean for the Schedule?

Your records become the argument. A hospital that required a nurse to stay has to be able to show what it tried before that, and the only thing that shows it is what you wrote down at the time. If the call order lives in the memory of whoever was on the phone, you have nothing to hand the nurse, the state, or your own counsel six weeks later.

The refusal right lands on a 25-bed hospital in three places.

**A callout is where this gets tested.** Call in this order: unit roster, then the PRN pool, then agency if you hold agreements, then a conversation about what the unit can carry. Write the call order down and follow it every time. An order you improvise is an order you cannot show later. [Handling nurse callouts](/blog/how-to-handle-nurse-callouts) has the full version, and [after-hours callout coverage](/blog/after-hours-callout-coverage-small-hospitals) covers the 2 a.m. case.

**Write four things per call, while you are making it.** Who you called, the time, what they said, and what you did next. That is the whole record, and it fits on the back of the assignment sheet. A [nurse schedule audit trail](/blog/nurse-schedule-audit-trail) is the durable version of the same four fields.

**The real fix is upstream.** A roster that depends on somebody staying over is one callout away from a compliance problem. Reducing that dependency is a schedule design question rather than a legal one, and [reducing nurse overtime](/blog/how-to-reduce-nurse-overtime) covers the patterns that create it.

## What to Do This Week

1. **Count how many shifts last month ended with somebody staying past their shift.** Timecards will tell you in twenty minutes. That count is the size of your exposure, and most managers guess it low.
2. **Pick one of those shifts and try to reconstruct who was asked and when.** If the answer lives only in somebody's memory, that is the gap to close first. It is also the cheapest one to close.
3. **Write the call order down on one page.** Unit roster, PRN pool, agency, then the conversation about what the unit can carry. Distribute it, because a sequence only you know is a sequence nobody else follows at 2 a.m.
4. **Decide the hard case before it happens.** Agree with your administrator now what you do when voluntary coverage fails and no exception applies. Deciding it at 7 p.m. is how hospitals end up giving an order they are not allowed to give.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Can a nurse be fired for refusing mandatory overtime?**

Not in a state that protects the refusal. Texas, Pennsylvania and Washington all prohibit retaliation against a nurse who declines. New York restricts the overtime itself without adding that protection, which is a reminder to check the two things separately. In a state with no such law, refusal is governed by the employment relationship and any union contract.

**Is mandatory overtime illegal in Texas?**

Yes, outside four narrow emergency exceptions, and a nurse who refuses is protected from retaliation. The practical reading for a nurse manager is that ordering someone to stay is not an available tool on an ordinary short night, however the conversation is worded.

**Are nursing assistants and techs covered?**

It depends on the state, and in Texas they are not. Chapter 258 covers registered nurses and licensed vocational nurses only. Oregon covers certified nursing assistants alongside nurses, and Washington covers every employee of a health care facility. Check the definition section of your own state's law rather than assuming it matches the nursing scope.

**Does a refusal right apply to on-call shifts?**

Not to prescheduled on-call time. Texas and New York both exclude it, so being called in from an on-call rotation is not mandatory overtime. New York separately bars an employer from using on-call time as a way around the limit.

**How many states restrict mandatory nurse overtime?**

Published counts say 16 or 18, and they disagree about which states belong. We read the statute or rule in seven states as of September 2026. Confirm your own state with its hospital association or your counsel before relying on any count.
