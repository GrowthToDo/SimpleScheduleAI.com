## Key Takeaways

- **Low census means fewer patients than the shift was staffed for.** At a small hospital the choices are to send nurses home, cancel a shift, or put someone on call.
- **Staffing can only go down to your minimums.** [Federal rules](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/42/485.631) require a registered nurse, clinical nurse specialist or licensed practical nurse on duty whenever a Critical Access Hospital has inpatients. [Texas rules](https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/texas/26-Tex-Admin-Code-SS-505-41) add an RN on duty in each building where patients are present, and your written staffing plan sets the minimums for each unit.
- **Who goes home first should be written down.** One small hospital's published policy asks for volunteers first, then rotates equally among the nurses on the unit. A practical order is overtime and agency hours first, then volunteers, then the rotation. Each pick is checked against the skills the remaining shift still needs.
- **A running count keeps the rotation honest.** Track low-census hours for each nurse across the year. Send home the nurse with the lowest count whom the shift can spare, so the same people do not keep losing pay.
- **Federal pay rules matter in two places.** A nurse on a fixed salary who is not paid overtime cannot have that salary cut for a day the hospital cancels. On-call time can count as hours worked depending on how restricted it is.

Low census is a normal part of running a small hospital. A few discharges, an empty swing bed and a quiet emergency department can leave a shift with more nurses than patients need. The hard part is deciding who goes home, and doing it the same way every time so it does not feel personal. This guide covers the staffing floors, a fair rotation with a worked count, and the federal pay rules a Director of Nursing should know. It explains the rules and is not legal advice; check pay questions with your HR lead or counsel.

## What Does Low Census Mean at a Critical Access Hospital?

Low census means the number of patients has dropped below what the shift was staffed for. The staffing company IntelyCare [defines it](https://www.intelycare.com/facilities/resources/5-ways-to-manage-a-low-census/) as "a significant decrease in the number of patients in your facility or floor, regardless of whether it's expected."

Losing three patients from a unit of eight is a big change for a small hospital. The choices are to send a nurse home early, cancel a scheduled shift before it starts, or put a nurse on call, meaning off the unit but ready to come back if patients arrive.

## How Low Can Nurse Staffing Go When Census Drops?

Only as low as your minimum staffing levels, the floor under every shift. Three rules set them for a Texas Critical Access Hospital, and they apply whatever the census is, as long as patients are present.

- **The federal floor.** [42 CFR 485.631(a)(5)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/42/485.631) requires that "a registered nurse, clinical nurse specialist, or licensed practical nurse is on duty whenever the CAH has one or more inpatients." Texas calls a licensed practical nurse a licensed vocational nurse (LVN).
- **The Texas floor.** [26 TAC 505.41(o)(2)(D)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/texas/26-Tex-Admin-Code-SS-505-41) adds a state rule. It says: "An RN shall be on duty in each building of a licensed hospital that contains at least one nursing unit where patients are present." So in Texas, a night with patients still needs a registered nurse in each of those buildings, even if an LVN alone would meet the federal rule. For a hospital with one building, that means one RN on duty whenever patients are present.
- **Your own plan.** [Texas Health and Safety Code 257.003](https://codes.findlaw.com/tx/health-and-safety-code/health-safety-sect-257-003) requires a written nurse staffing policy. Its purpose is to "ensure that an adequate number and skill mix of nurses are available to meet the level of patient care needed." The minimums in that plan are the floor your hospital set for itself. Our guide to [nurse staffing ratios at a critical access hospital](/blog/nurse-staffing-ratios-critical-access-hospital) covers the plan in more detail.

Skill mix, meaning the mix of skills the patients still need, is the other limit. Before anyone goes home, check that the shift still has a charge nurse and any skills such as emergency department coverage.

## Who Goes Home First When Census Drops?

The order should be written in your policy, so the charge nurse is not deciding from scratch at 7 PM. Two published examples show different shapes.

A small Washington hospital's [nurse staffing policy](https://doh.wa.gov/sites/default/files/legacy/Documents/2300/HospPolicies/FerryCountyNS.pdf) puts volunteers first. The manager "will first ask for volunteers to take time off." If no one volunteers, it rotates low census "equitably among nurses assigned to each area by shift, subject to competence, ability, and availability."

Larger hospitals with union contracts can set a longer order. A [union guide to one Washington hospital's contract](https://www.seiu1199nw.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/10/bb-100924-seh.pdf) lists who is cancelled first: agency and travel staff, then nurses on overtime, then volunteers, then nurses working extra shifts, then per diem, part-time and full-time staff. The union order is one contract's terms, not a rule, and a 20-nurse hospital may not have that many groups to cut through.

At a small hospital the practical order can be shorter. Cancel overtime and agency hours first if you have any, ask for volunteers, then rotate. A hospital with a [float pool](/blog/float-pool-nursing-small-hospitals) can also move a nurse to another unit instead of sending anyone home.

## How Do You Rotate Low Census Fairly on a Small Roster?

Keep a running count of low-census hours for each nurse across the year. When someone has to go home and nobody volunteers, send home the nurse with the lowest count whom the shift can spare, then add the hours to that nurse's total.

Nurse C keeps the lowest count only because the shift needed a charge nurse. On a shift with a second charge nurse, Nurse C goes home first. Nurse B and Nurse F both have 12 hours, so your policy should say how a tie breaks, for example by whoever was sent home longest ago.

Decide too whether a volunteer's hours count toward the total. We would count them. If they do not count, a nurse who volunteers often keeps a low total and is also picked first in the rotation, so that nurse loses time twice.

The count is the same idea as tracking weekends and holidays per nurse, which our guide to a [fair nurse schedule at a small hospital](/blog/fair-nurse-schedule-small-hospital) explains. Without a count, nobody can show whose turn it is. Lost shifts that keep landing on the same nurses are a fairness problem, which our guide to [nurse burnout and scheduling](/blog/nurse-burnout-scheduling-cah) also covers.

## Do You Have to Pay a Nurse Sent Home for Low Census?

For hourly nurses, pay for a cancelled shift is set by hospital policy. We found no Texas law requiring reporting-time pay, meaning a minimum payment for a worker who shows up and is sent home early. FindLaw, a legal reference site, [says](https://www.findlaw.com/state/texas-law/texas-wage-and-hour-laws.html) that "Texas doesn't require reporting time pay when a worker shows up but is sent home early." FindLaw is a secondary source, so confirm it with your counsel.

Policies still handle pay in different ways. The staffing company Cascade [lists the options](https://cascadestaff.com/understanding-low-census-pay/) as partial or minimum pay guarantees, a low-census paid time off (PTO) bank, on-call or standby pay, and PTO or unpaid time off. Whatever your hospital chooses, write it down, so nurses know before the shift whether a send-home costs them pay or PTO.

## Can You Dock a Salaried Nurse for a Low Census Day?

It depends on how the nurse is paid. A nurse on a fixed salary who is not paid overtime cannot have that salary cut for a day the hospital itself cancels. The federal salary rule is [29 CFR 541.602](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/29/541.602). It says an employee is not paid on a salary basis "if deductions from the employee's predetermined compensation are made for absences occasioned by the employer or by the operating requirements of the business." It adds: "If the employee is ready, willing and able to work, deductions may not be made for time when work is not available."

The salary rule applies only to nurses who are exempt from overtime and paid a salary. Under [29 CFR 541.301](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/29/541.301), registered nurses "generally meet the duties requirements for the learned professional exemption," the overtime exemption for professionals, while licensed practical nurses "generally do not qualify." If your hospital pays registered nurses by the hour, the overtime rules in our guides to [reducing nurse overtime](/blog/how-to-reduce-nurse-overtime) and [Texas nursing overtime](/blog/texas-nursing-overtime-compliance-cah) apply instead. Whether a salaried nurse's PTO can be used for the cancelled day is a question for your HR lead or counsel.

## Is On-Call During Low Census Paid Time?

It depends on how tied down the nurse is. The federal rule on on-call time, [29 CFR 785.17](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/29/785.17), says an employee who must stay "on the employer's premises or so close thereto that he cannot use the time effectively for his own purposes is working while 'on call'." An employee who only has to say where they can be reached "is not working while on call."

So the answer depends on the restrictions your hospital sets. A nurse sent home on call who only has to say where they can be reached is not counted as hours worked under that rule. A nurse told to wait in the break room is. Some policies pay a standby rate, a set hourly amount for being on call, either way; it is one of the options on the Cascade list above. Write the rate into the policy, and put the hours of a nurse called back in on the schedule so they count toward the 40-hour overtime week. Our guide to [handling nurse callouts](/blog/how-to-handle-nurse-callouts) covers bringing an on-call nurse back.

## What Should a Written Low Census Policy Say?

A short policy answers the questions the charge nurse would otherwise answer on the spot.

1. **Who decides,** and who the charge nurse calls if the decision is disputed.
2. **The floor,** meaning the minimum staffing from your written plan and the Texas rule that an RN stays on duty in each building with patients.
3. **The order:** overtime and agency hours first, then volunteers, then the rotation.
4. **How the rotation works:** the running count, the tie-break, and whether volunteer hours count.
5. **Pay and PTO:** whether a send-home is unpaid, paid from PTO, or paid at a minimum.
6. **On-call:** when it applies, the standby rate, and how call-back hours are paid.
7. **The record:** who updates the count after each send-home, and where it is kept.

## What to Do This Week

1. **Find your current low-census rule, or write one.** If the order lives only in the charge nurses' heads, write the seven points above on one page this week.
2. **Start the count.** List every nurse. Start each at zero from a set date, such as January 1, and say so in the policy, so a nurse with missing records is not first in line.
3. **Check your floors.** Read the minimums in your written staffing plan and confirm every unit can still put an RN on duty in each building with patients after a send-home.
4. **Decide the pay question in writing.** Unpaid, PTO or minimum pay, and the standby rate for on-call time. Ask HR how salaried nurses are handled before the next cancellation.
5. **Brief your charge nurses.** Walk them through the order, the count and the tie-break, so the next low-census call is the same one any charge nurse would make.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### The basics

**Q: What does low census mean in a hospital?**

Low census means there are fewer patients than the shift was staffed for. The choices are sending a nurse home early, cancelling a scheduled shift, or putting a nurse on call until patients arrive.

**Q: Who gets sent home first for low census?**

That depends on your written policy. A practical order is to cancel overtime and agency hours, ask for volunteers, then rotate among the nurses on the unit, skipping anyone the shift needs for a skill such as charge or emergency coverage.

**Q: How low can a Texas Critical Access Hospital staff during low census?**

A licensed nurse must be on duty whenever there are inpatients under federal rules, and Texas requires an RN on duty in each building where patients are present. Your written staffing plan sets the unit minimums on top of those rules.

### Pay and policy

**Q: Do hospitals have to pay nurses sent home for low census in Texas?**

We found no Texas law requiring reporting-time pay for a worker sent home early, so pay for hourly nurses is set by hospital policy. Options policies use include unpaid time, PTO, a minimum pay guarantee or standby pay. Confirm the legal position with your counsel.

**Q: Can a hospital dock a salaried nurse for a low census day?**

A nurse on a fixed salary who is not paid overtime cannot have that salary cut for a day the hospital itself cancels. Federal rule 29 CFR 541.602 says deductions for absences caused by the employer's operating needs mean the nurse no longer counts as paid a true salary. Hourly nurses are paid for hours worked instead.

**Q: Does on-call time during low census count as hours worked?**

It depends on the restrictions. Under 29 CFR 785.17, a nurse who must stay on or near the premises is working, while a nurse who only has to say where they can be reached is not. Some policies pay a standby rate either way.
