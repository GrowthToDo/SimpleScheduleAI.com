## Key Takeaways

- **A nurse on-call schedule names who can be called in if the shift needs another nurse.** The on-call nurse is off the unit and reachable. The on-call nurse is always in addition to the nurse on duty, never a replacement.
- **On-call time at home is usually not hours worked.** Under [29 CFR 785.17](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/29/785.17), a nurse who only has to say where they can be reached is not working. A nurse kept on the premises, or so close that the time is not their own, is working. The hours worked after a call-in are always paid.
- **A standby payment for being on call goes into the overtime rate.** [29 CFR 778.223](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/29/778.223) says the payment must be included in the regular rate (the hourly rate overtime is calculated from). The rule holds even when the on-call hours themselves are not hours worked.
- **In Texas, your staffing plan must say how on-call time is used.** The [Texas hospital licensing rule](https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/texas/26-Tex-Admin-Code-SS-505-41) requires it. Texas also bars a hospital from using on-call time as a substitute for mandatory overtime.
- **Spread on-call nights with a running count.** On a 12-nurse unit with one on-call slot every night, a 30-day month gives each nurse 2.5 nights on average. A count carried month to month keeps the extra night rotating.

## What Is a Nurse On-Call Schedule at a Small Hospital?

A nurse on-call schedule lists, for each night or weekend, the nurse who stays reachable and comes in if the unit needs another nurse. The on-call nurse is at home or nearby, not on the unit. Reasons to call the nurse in include a new admission, a sicker patient, or a transfer that needs a second nurse on the unit.

On call is different from on duty. [42 CFR 485.631(a)(5)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/42/485.631) requires a registered nurse, clinical nurse specialist or licensed practical nurse on duty whenever the hospital has one or more inpatients. [Texas rules](https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/texas/26-Tex-Admin-Code-SS-505-41) add an RN on duty in each building with patients. Neither rule defines "on duty." We read it as working on the unit, so an on-call nurse at home does not meet either rule. The on-call nurse is an extra nurse, added to the one on duty.

A [per diem nurse](/blog/what-is-per-diem-nursing) can take call too. Physicians and other practitioners have their own call rules, which our guide to [medical staff scheduling at a small hospital](/blog/physician-medical-staff-scheduling-small-hospital) covers. This post is about nurses.

## Does On-Call Time Count as Hours Worked for Nurses?

On-call time at home usually does not count as hours worked, the paid time that also counts toward overtime. The federal rule, [29 CFR 785.17](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/29/785.17), sets the test. An employee is working while on call if they must stay on the premises, or so close that they cannot use the time "effectively for his own purposes," in the rule's words. An employee who only has to "leave word at his home or with company officials where he may be reached is not working while on call."

The Department of Labor's health care [Fact Sheet #53](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/whd/fact-sheets/53-healthcare-hours-worked) adds a caution. An employee who carries a phone or can be reached by message "is not working (in most cases) while on-call," the fact sheet says. It adds a warning: "Additional constraints on the employee's freedom could require this time to be compensated."

A very short response time, or a rule that the nurse cannot leave town, could be that kind of constraint. Neither 785.17 nor the fact sheet gives a number of minutes. The fact sheet's one example is an assisted living facility, not a hospital. Its four hourly LPNs take turns on call, a week at a time, carrying a cell phone and staying within 45 minutes of the facility. The LPNs are paid for time answering calls and time back at work, and the Labor Department says that complies. The 45 minutes is that facility's rule, not a federal standard.

## How Should On-Call Nurses Be Paid When They Are Called In?

A nurse called in is paid for every hour worked, and those hours count toward the 40-hour overtime week. Hospital overtime is generally due after 40 hours in a workweek, at 1.5 times the regular rate. Two other pay choices are hospital policy:

- **A standby rate.** Some policies pay a set amount for each on-call night, whether or not the nurse is called in.
- **A call-back minimum.** Some policies guarantee a minimum number of paid hours when a nurse is called in, even for a short call.

Federal law does not set a call-back minimum or a standby rate; those are hospital policy. One published example is the [University of Rochester on-call pay policy](https://www.rochester.edu/policies/policy/on-call-pay), which covers hourly staff. One division (Division 50) pays $16.00 for 8 to 11 hours on call and $24.00 for 12 to 24 hours. Other divisions pay $12.50 and $18.50. A nurse called in is paid for the hours worked or at least 4 hours at the regular rate, whichever is greater, on top of the on-call payment. Rochester is a large New York academic medical center, not a small hospital, so treat these rates as one example and not a norm.

### Does standby pay change the overtime rate?

Yes, a standby payment is part of the regular rate used to work out overtime. [29 CFR 778.223](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/29/778.223) gives the example of a payment for each on-call period, where the employee can come and go and the on-call hours are not hours worked. It says that payment "must therefore be included in the employee's regular rate." Ask payroll to confirm that its overtime calculation includes the standby pay.

Call-back minimums follow a different rule, [29 CFR 778.221](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/29/778.221). If a minimum pays for more hours than the nurse worked, the extra paid hours can be left out of the regular rate. That exception does not apply when the call-in was arranged in advance. A planned on-call rotation may count as arranged in advance, so ask your payroll provider before leaving those hours out.

## What Does Texas Law Say About Nurse On-Call Time?

Texas law defines on-call time, keeps it out of mandatory overtime, and requires the hospital's staffing plan to say how it is used. Four rules matter.

1. **The definition.** [Texas Health and Safety Code chapter 258](https://texas.public.law/statutes/tex._health_and_safety_code_section_258.001) defines on-call time. It is "time spent by a nurse who is not working but who is compensated for availability," and the [Texas hospital licensing rule](https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/texas/26-Tex-Admin-Code-SS-505-41) repeats it.
2. **On-call is not mandatory overtime.** Mandatory overtime means requiring a nurse to work hours beyond the schedule, and a Texas nurse may refuse it. The rule says mandatory overtime "does not include prescheduled on-call time," meaning on-call time put on the schedule in advance. So being scheduled on call is not mandatory overtime. We read that to cover a call-in during a scheduled on-call night too, but the rule does not say so directly.
3. **On-call cannot replace mandatory overtime.** The same rule says: "A hospital may not use on-call time as a substitute for mandatory overtime."
   In plain terms, on-call time cannot become a way to make a nurse work extra hours the hospital could not require directly. Our guide to [mandatory nurse overtime by state](/blog/mandatory-nurse-overtime-by-state) covers the overtime limits themselves.
4. **The staffing plan.** The licensing rule says the hospital's official nurse staffing plan shall "include how on-call time will be used."

So in Texas the on-call rotation belongs in the written staffing plan. The plan should say who takes call, how often, and how fast the nurse must respond. Our guide to [Texas nursing overtime compliance](/blog/texas-nursing-overtime-compliance-cah) covers the plan alongside on-call and callback records.

The definition says on-call time is "compensated for availability," so one question is worth asking your counsel or administrator. If your on-call nurses get no standby pay, is that time on-call time under chapter 258? If not, calling those nurses in could be treated as mandatory overtime, which a nurse may refuse. We checked chapter 258 and the hospital licensing rule and found no Texas rule that caps how often a nurse can be on call. We did not check the Texas Board of Nursing rules.

## Does CMS Set a Response Time for On-Call Nurses?

Not in the federal regulations for Critical Access Hospitals. We searched those regulations and found no response time for on-call nurses; we did not read CMS's separate guidance for surveyors. The only on-call timing rule in the regulations is for practitioners. [42 CFR 485.618(d)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/42/485.618) requires a doctor, physician assistant, nurse practitioner or clinical nurse specialist to be "on call and immediately available by telephone or radio contact, and available on site within ... 30 minutes." Hospitals in some very remote areas get 60 minutes. That rule covers emergency practitioners, not staff nurses. A narrow temporary exception lets an emergency-trained RN fill the role at some remote hospitals of 10 beds or fewer.

So the response time for an on-call nurse is your hospital's choice. Write it into the on-call policy and the staffing plan, for example "on the unit within 30 minutes of the call." A very short response time ties the nurse down more. Under 29 CFR 785.17, that can make on-call time count as hours worked.

## How Many On-Call Nights Should Each Nurse Carry on a 12-Nurse Unit?

On a 12-nurse unit with one on-call slot every night, each nurse carries 2.5 on-call nights a month on average. Here is the worked example. It is a made-up unit to show the math, not a real hospital or a customer result.

**The unit.** A medical-surgical unit at a 25-bed hospital has 12 RNs. One RN is always on duty. A second RN is on call every night, from 7 PM to 7 AM, in case census or acuity needs another nurse.

**The monthly load.** A 30-day month has 30 on-call nights. Split across 12 nurses, that is 2.5 nights each, so six nurses carry 3 nights and six carry 2. If 4 of the 12 work only night shifts and stay off the on-call list, 8 nurses share the 30 nights. That is 3.75 nights each.

**The limits.** Suppose your policy allows one on-call night per nurse per week, and one weekend on-call night per nurse per month. A weekend night here means a Saturday or Sunday night. Each limit sets a minimum number of eligible nurses, meaning nurses allowed to take call on that unit.

With all 12 nurses eligible, both limits work. With only 8 eligible, any month with more than 8 weekend nights cannot be covered. So the policy has to change that month: either allow a second weekend night for some nurses, or bring a night-shift nurse onto the list.

**The pay when a nurse is called in.** Take one nurse from the unit. That nurse works three 12-hour shifts that week, which is 36 hours, at an illustrative $40 an hour. The nurse also holds one on-call night, within the weekly limit, at an illustrative $20 standby payment. The nurse is called in that night and works 7 hours.

- Hours worked: 36 + 7 = 43, so 3 hours fall past 40. The rest of the on-call night is not counted, because the nurse only had to be reachable.
- Straight-time pay, meaning every hour at the normal rate: 43 hours at $40 is $1,720, plus the $20 standby payment, for $1,740.
- Regular rate, with the standby pay included: $1,740 divided by 43 hours is $40.47.
- Overtime premium: straight-time pay already covers all 43 hours once, so overtime adds the extra half. Half of $40.47, times 3 hours, is $60.71. The week's total is $1,800.71.

If payroll leaves the standby pay out, the premium is half of $40, times 3 hours, which is $60.00. The nurse is underpaid $0.71 that week. The amount is small, but it repeats every week an on-call nurse works past 40 hours. The example assumes the usual 40-hour workweek; a hospital using the 8-and-80 system (overtime past 8 hours in a day or 80 hours in 14 days) figures overtime differently.

The bigger risk is how tightly the policy restricts the on-call nurse. Suppose the policy ties the nurse down so they cannot use the time for their own purposes. Then the whole 12-hour on-call night can count as hours worked. The week becomes 36 + 12 = 48 hours, with 8 of them past 40. Which side of that line a policy falls on depends on the facts, so check it with counsel.

## How Do You Spread On-Call Fairly Across a Small Nursing Staff?

Keep a running count of on-call nights for each nurse, and carry it from month to month. Thirty nights do not split evenly across 12 nurses, so someone carries 3 instead of 2. The count makes sure the nurses who carried 3 this month carry 2 next month. Start every nurse at zero from a set date, such as January 1, so nobody starts behind because of old records.

A few rules keep the count honest:

1. **Ask for volunteers first.** A nurse may want the extra nights for the standby pay. Count their volunteered nights like any other.
2. **Count weekend and holiday nights separately.** A Saturday on call costs a nurse more than a Tuesday. Track them in their own column, the same way our guide to [fair holiday rotation](/blog/fair-holiday-rotation-nurses-cah) tracks holidays.
3. **Count call-ins too.** A nurse who is called in on most on-call nights is working extra shifts. If the same nurse keeps getting called in, look at whether the base schedule is short.
4. **Protect the morning after.** Decide in the policy what happens when a nurse is called in overnight and is scheduled at 7 AM. The policy can move the next shift, or let the nurse start late after rest. Our guide to [low census](/blog/low-census-small-hospital) covers the related case of a nurse sent home on call when patients drop.

The count is the same idea our guide to a [fair nurse schedule at a small hospital](/blog/fair-nurse-schedule-small-hospital) uses for weekends. Without a count, nobody can show whose turn it is, and call can land on the few nurses who never say no.

## What to Do This Week

1. **Count last quarter's on-call nights.** List every nurse and how many on-call nights and weekend nights each one carried. The gaps between nurses show where call has been landing.
2. **Read your on-call policy.** Check that it states where the nurse must be, how fast they must respond, the standby rate, and any call-back minimum.
3. **Ask payroll one question.** Is standby pay included in the regular rate when an on-call nurse works past 40 hours? If not, fix it before the next pay period.
4. **Open your nurse staffing plan.** In Texas, confirm it says how on-call time will be used. If it does not, add the rotation, the limits and the response time.
5. **Set the limits and start the count.** Pick a weekly limit and a monthly weekend limit, check that you have enough eligible nurses to meet them, and start each nurse's count from a set date.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### The basics

**Q: Is on-call the same as PRN?**

No, on-call and PRN are different things. On-call is a duty on the schedule: a nurse stays reachable for a set period. PRN, or per diem, is a type of employment: a nurse works shifts as needed without a set schedule. A PRN nurse can take call.

**Q: Does an on-call nurse count as the nurse on duty?**

We read the rules as saying an on-call nurse at home does not count as the nurse on duty. Federal rules require a licensed nurse on duty whenever a Critical Access Hospital has inpatients, and Texas requires an RN on duty in each building with patients.

### Pay and law

**Q: Is a nurse paid for on-call time at home?**

A nurse who only has to stay reachable is usually not working under 29 CFR 785.17, so that time is not hours worked. A hospital can still pay a standby rate. Every hour worked after a call-in is paid and counts toward the 40-hour overtime week.

**Q: Do on-call hours count toward the 40-hour week?**

The hours a nurse works after a call-in count toward the 40-hour overtime week. The hours at home waiting for a call usually do not, unless the policy restricts the nurse so much that the time is not their own.

**Q: Does federal law require a call-back minimum for nurses?**

Federal law does not require a call-back minimum or a standby rate; both are hospital policy. Federal law does require pay for every hour actually worked, with overtime past 40 hours in the workweek.

**Q: Can a Texas hospital require a nurse to take call?**

We found no Texas rule that stops a hospital from putting nurses on call in advance, because prescheduled on-call time is not mandatory overtime. Texas does bar a hospital from using on-call time as a substitute for mandatory overtime, so check your rotation with counsel.
