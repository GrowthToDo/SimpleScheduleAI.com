## Key Takeaways

- **An island shift is one scheduled shift with days off on both sides.** Shift-work research recommends against it. A [2021 study of nurse schedules](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8209316/) lists "no single working day between days off" among the recommendations it checked schedules against.
- **Night nurses pay the most for an island shift.** An island night is always a first night back after days off. [NIOSH training for nurses](https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/work-hour-training-for-nurses/longhours/mod5/05.html) asks employers of permanent night staff to add patient-safety measures on that first night back.
- **A block of three nights has one first night back for three nights of work.** An island shift has one first night back for one night of work.
- **Island shifts come from filling gaps one night at a time.** Nights patched slot by slot, and callouts covered by a nurse who is off on both sides, create island shifts nobody chose.
- **The fix is mostly a check.** Assign nights in runs of two or three. Read every nurse's row for an off, on, off pattern before posting. On every callout, check the day before and the day after.

Consider a night nurse at a 25-bed hospital whose posted week reads: off Sunday and Monday, work Tuesday night, off Wednesday and Thursday, then work Friday and Saturday nights. Tuesday is an island shift. On paper the week looks light. For the nurse, Tuesday night comes after two days of sleeping at night. Wednesday and Thursday go to recovering and sleeping at night again. Friday night is then a second first night back in the same week.

## What Is an Island Shift in Nursing?

An island shift is a single scheduled shift with days off on both sides: off, on, off. "Island shift" is an informal scheduling term, not a research term. Shift-work research describes the same pattern as a "single working day between days off" and recommends avoiding it.

The clearest recent example is a [2021 study in Safety and Health at Work](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8209316/) by Lee and Jeong. The authors checked the schedules of 182 hospital nurses in South Korea against a list of scheduling recommendations. One item on that list reads "No single working day between days off." The list was adapted from ergonomic guidance by the shift-work researchers Knauth and Hornberger.

An island shift can be a day, evening or night shift. The pattern costs the most on nights, for the reasons in the next section. No study we found measured harm from island shifts directly. The advice rests on shift-work guidance and how body clocks adjust.

## Why Are Island Shifts Harder on Night Nurses?

An island night shift gives the body clock no time to adjust. The nurse comes off days off, stays awake through one night, then goes straight back to days off.

A NIOSH [training module on night and evening shifts](https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/work-hour-training-for-nurses/longhours/mod9/02.html) tells nurses: "You are working against your circadian clock, which is strongly influenced by the sun's light-dark cycle." The same page warns that night nurses "might get less sleep and poorer-quality sleep than those working the day shift."

Days off make the adjustment harder still. A [2012 review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3630978/) by sleep researchers at Rush University Medical Center found that "the circadian rhythms of real night-shift workers rarely shift to align with their new sleep schedules." One reason the authors give: workers "sleep at conventional times on days off." An island night is the extreme case: one night of work with nighttime sleep on both sides.

NIOSH's [guidelines for designing work schedules](https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/work-hour-training-for-nurses/longhours/mod5/05.html) name the risk point directly. Where permanent night shifts are necessary, NIOSH advises employers to "create measures to maintain patient safety during their first night back after several days off." A block of three nights has one first night back for three nights of work. An island shift has one first night back for one night of work.

## Why Do Island Shifts Show Up on Small Hospital Schedules?

Island shifts appear when nights are filled one open slot at a time instead of assigned in runs. A manager covering the last gaps in a schedule picks whoever is free that night, without reading the days on either side. A callout covered by a nurse who is off the day before and the day after creates an island shift too.

Nights are the hardest shifts to fill at a 25-bed hospital, as our guide to [night shift nurse schedule coverage](/blog/night-shift-nurse-schedule-coverage) explains. So nights can be the gaps left at the end of a schedule build, when the person building it is filling slots one at a time.

The person building the schedule may never have been taught to look for the pattern. A [2024 study in BMC Nursing](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11057102/) interviewed 24 nurses responsible for building schedules at three Australian hospitals. The researchers found these schedulers "under prepared, unaware of fatigue and safety guidelines" set by health and nursing authorities.

Island shifts were common in the South Korean study: 115 of the 182 nurses had at least one island shift across 13 weeks of schedules. Island nights were the rarest kind: 29 of the 255 island shifts the study counted. The study's hospitals ran three-shift rotations (day, evening and night), so the counts will not transfer cleanly to a unit running 12-hour shifts.

## How Do You Schedule Island Shifts Out of a Night Rotation?

Assign nights in runs and read each nurse's row for an off, on, off pattern before the schedule posts. NIOSH points the same way: keep runs of night shifts short on rotating schedules, and "consider two rest days after three consecutive 12-hour shifts."

Building in runs means each assignment is a block of two or three nights. The Lee and Jeong recommendation list caps runs at three nights in a row. A nurse given a block cannot be given an island shift inside it. Over two weeks, a nurse built in runs might work Monday to Wednesday nights, take Thursday to Saturday off, then work Sunday to Tuesday nights.

Then read the schedule one nurse at a time. Look across each row for a single shift with an off day on each side. When you find an island shift, try two fixes in order. First, move the shift next to that nurse's other nights. Second, give the night to a nurse whose run it would extend. If neither fix works, ask the nurse before the schedule posts.

After the swap, Nurse B works Monday to Wednesday nights and Nurse A works Thursday to Saturday nights, three in a row each. Every night from Monday to Saturday still has exactly one of the two nurses on, and each nurse still works three nights.

Some island shifts come from the nurse's own day-off requests. A nurse who has Monday off and asks for Wednesday and Thursday off may be left with Tuesday night alone. Before posting, ask that nurse one question: "Your requested days off leave Tuesday night on its own. Do you want to keep Tuesday night, or should I try to swap it for a night next to your other nights?"

## How Do You Avoid an Island Shift When Covering a Callout?

Before calling anyone, look at each candidate's schedule for the day before and the day after the open night. A nurse who worked last night, or works tomorrow night, can cover without creating an island shift. A nurse who is off on both sides cannot.

The check takes one glance per name. A nurse with one or two nights already in a run next to the open night can add the open night and stay within three in a row. A nurse whose two-night run starts tomorrow can start a night early, making three. Either fill extends a run instead of creating a new first night back.

Sometimes the only nurse available is off on both sides. At a 25-bed hospital that can happen. In Texas, Health and Safety Code Chapter 258 bars a hospital from making a nurse work extra hours or extra days beyond the posted schedule. [Section 258.002](https://texas.public.law/statutes/tex._health_and_safety_code_section_258.002) defines extra hours or days added to a nurse's posted schedule as mandatory overtime, and [Section 258.003](https://texas.public.law/statutes/tex._health_and_safety_code_section_258.003) prohibits mandatory overtime. Narrow emergency exceptions exist, and the nurse may say no. So an off-duty nurse covering an island night is doing the unit a favor. Say plainly that the shift is an island shift when you ask.

A simple note of who covered island nights shows, over a few months, whether the same nurse always says yes. ## What to Do This Week

1. Pull your current night schedule and mark every off, on, off pattern in each nurse's row.
2. For each island shift you find, check whether the night can move next to that nurse's other nights, or swap with a nurse whose run it would extend.
3. Set a rule for your next build: nights assigned in runs of two or three, with two days off after three 12-hour nights, as NIOSH suggests.
4. Add one step to your callout routine: check the day before and the day after the open night for every nurse you plan to call.
5. Tell a nurse when a callout fill would be an island shift, and keep a note of who said yes.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: What is an island shift in nursing?**

An island shift is a single scheduled shift with days off on both sides: off, on, off. Shift-work research describes the pattern as a "single working day between days off" and recommends avoiding it. An island shift can fall on days, evenings or nights. The pattern costs the most on nights, because every island night is a first night back after days off.

**Q: Is one night shift between days off worse than a block of nights?**

For the same number of nights worked, yes. A block of three nights has one first night back for three nights of work. An island shift has one first night back for one night of work. NIOSH training for nurses asks employers of permanent night staff to add patient-safety measures on the first night back after several days off.

**Q: Can a Texas hospital require a nurse to cover an island shift?**

Yes if the shift is already on the posted schedule. No if it is added on top. Texas Health and Safety Code Chapter 258 bars a hospital from making a nurse work extra hours or extra days beyond the posted schedule. Narrow emergency exceptions exist, and the nurse may say no.

**Q: How many night shifts in a row should a nurse work?**

Runs of two or three nights. NIOSH advises keeping runs of night shifts short on rotating schedules and suggests two rest days after three 12-hour shifts in a row. The 2021 Lee and Jeong recommendation list caps runs at three nights in a row.
