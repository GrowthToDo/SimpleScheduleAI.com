## Key Takeaways

- A PRN pool is a group of nurses who work as needed (PRN means "as needed"), with no guaranteed hours. At a small hospital it is how you cover callouts without overtime or an agency.
- Set it up in four steps: recruit the right nurses, get each one credentialed and oriented, collect their available dates before the schedule is built, and write down the order you call them in.
- Check each nurse's hours for the week before you offer a shift. For the same 12-hour shift, a PRN nurse already at 36 hours is paid for 16 hours: 4 hours at normal pay plus 8 overtime hours paid at time and a half. A nurse at 28 hours is paid for 12.
- In Texas, [Health and Safety Code Chapter 258](https://law.justia.com/codes/texas/health-and-safety-code/title-4/subtitle-b/chapter-258/) bars a hospital from requiring a nurse to work extra hours or extra days beyond their schedule. A PRN shift is an offer the nurse can decline.
- Scheduling software can hold the dates, apply them when the schedule is built and rank who to call. Credentialing and timekeeping belong to other systems.

## What Is a PRN Pool at a Critical Access Hospital?

A PRN pool is a group of nurses who work as needed, with no guaranteed schedule and usually no benefits. PRN is short for the Latin pro re nata, "as the situation requires." PRN nurses cover callouts, leave and busy days. PRN and per diem describe the same arrangement; our guide to [what per diem nursing is](/blog/what-is-per-diem-nursing) explains the terms and the pay.

At a 25-bed hospital, the PRN pool does the job a float pool does at a larger one. A small hospital rarely has enough nurses to keep a dedicated float team, so the PRN list becomes the bench. Our [float pool guide for small hospitals](/blog/float-pool-nursing-small-hospitals) covers why.

The New York State Nurses Association makes the case for an internal pool plainly. Its [position statement on floating](https://www.nysna.org/position-statement-floating) names one answer as the best fix for emergency staffing. It calls it "the establishment of an internal pool of competent personnel whose credentials have been evaluated and who have been oriented and trained to the facility's units, supplies, equipment, and current policies." That sentence is also a checklist, and the next two sections follow it.

## Who Should You Recruit Into a PRN Pool?

Recruit nurses who already know your hospital or your kind of patients. They need the least orientation and are the most likely to say yes to a short-notice shift. Four groups are worth asking first:

- **Nurses who left your staff on good terms,** for a move, a family change or a job with regular hours elsewhere.
- **Nurses at nearby hospitals** who want extra shifts on their days off.
- **Recently retired nurses** who want a few shifts a month rather than none.
- **Your own part-time nurses** who would take more hours if asked.

When you ask, be plain: which shifts could they take, how many a month, and would they keep their certifications current with you. Be honest about which shifts you most need filled. If your gaps are nights and weekends, say so. A nurse who only wants weekday days is still worth having, but you should know that before you count on them for Saturday night.

## What Does a PRN Nurse Need Before a First Shift?

A PRN nurse needs the same credential checks and unit orientation as a staff nurse before working a first shift. That means license verification, the certifications your unit requires, and orientation to your units, equipment and policies. The NYSNA wording above names all three: credentials evaluated, oriented, and trained.

This work belongs to HR, your credentialing process and your unit educator. It is also the step most likely to stall a new PRN nurse for weeks, so start it as soon as someone says yes. A PRN nurse who is not cleared cannot be put on the schedule, however many dates they offer.

Keep a simple record of which units each PRN nurse is cleared for: a spreadsheet with one row per nurse and one column per unit is enough. A nurse oriented to med-surg is not automatically ready for the emergency department. That record decides who you can call for which shift.

## How Do You Collect PRN Availability Before the Schedule Is Built?

Collect each PRN nurse's available dates before you build the schedule, by a fixed deadline, for the whole schedule period. Then the dates are in hand when you plan coverage, and you are not phoning around after the schedule is posted.

Pick a deadline a few days before you start building, and send one reminder. Ask for specific dates and shifts, not "I'm usually free on weekends." A general answer cannot be put on a schedule. Keep the dates in one place, whether that is a spreadsheet or your scheduling system, not in a string of text messages.

Then check who has not replied. A missing reply means you do not know if that nurse is available, and you should not count on them. In the 30-plus nurse manager interviews we ran before building SimpleScheduleAI, collecting availability took a typical nurse manager about 1 to 1.5 hours a week; our [nurse manager scheduling time breakdown](/blog/nurse-manager-scheduling-time-breakdown) shows the full split. A fixed deadline is how that hour stops spilling into the rest of the week.

## In What Order Should You Call PRN Nurses When a Shift Opens?

Call nurses who can work the shift at straight time first, and leave overtime and agency for last. Straight time means normal pay, because the shift keeps them at or under 40 hours this week: a part-time staff nurse with room in their week, or a PRN nurse who offered that date. Next come nurses who would go into overtime, including full-time staff on a day off. An agency comes last. Write the order down so anyone covering the phone uses the same one.

The reason for the order is cost and familiarity. Straight time costs less than overtime, and your own staff and PRN nurses already know the unit. An agency nurse costs the most and knows the unit least.

Only call PRN nurses who offered that date and are cleared for that unit. Calling everyone on the list wastes the time you do not have at 5 a.m. Our guides to [handling nurse callouts](/blog/how-to-handle-nurse-callouts) and [after-hours callout coverage](/blog/after-hours-callout-coverage-small-hospitals) cover the call itself and what to record afterward. Holidays strain the pool most; see [covering callouts from Thanksgiving to New Year](/blog/december-nurse-callout-surge-cah).

## When Does a PRN Shift Push a Nurse Into Overtime?

A PRN shift pushes a nurse into overtime when it takes their hours for the workweek past 40. Under the federal rule most hospitals use, overtime is generally owed after 40 hours in a workweek, at one and a half times the nurse's normal hourly pay ([U.S. Department of Labor, Fact Sheet #54](https://www.dol.gov/agencies/whd/fact-sheets/54-healthcare-overtime)). Count every hour the nurse worked for your hospital that week, not only the hours on your unit.

Here is an illustrative example, not a result from any hospital. A 12-hour Saturday night shift is open. Five PRN nurses are on your list, and the workweek runs Monday to Sunday.

| PRN nurse | Offered Saturday night? | Hours already worked this week | Hours after the shift | Overtime hours |
| --------- | ----------------------- | ------------------------------ | --------------------- | -------------- |
| Nurse A   | No                      | 12                             | Not eligible          | Not eligible   |
| Nurse B   | Yes                     | 0                              | 12                    | 0              |
| Nurse C   | Yes                     | 12                             | 24                    | 0              |
| Nurse D   | Yes                     | 28                             | 40                    | 0              |
| Nurse E   | Yes                     | 36                             | 48                    | 8              |

Nurse A is out, because they did not offer the date. Nurses B, C and D can all work it at straight time. Nurse E can work it too, but 8 of the 12 hours become overtime.

Now the pay. Nurse D is paid for 12 hours. Nurse E works 4 hours at normal pay and 8 hours at time and a half, which is one and a half times normal pay. Those 8 overtime hours are paid as 12, so Nurse E is paid for 16 hours in total. The same 12 hours on the floor cost a third more. Multiply 12 and 16 by your own hourly rate to see the dollar difference.

The hours check belongs next to the call list for this reason. A PRN nurse who worked two shifts elsewhere in the building this week can look fresh on your list and still cost overtime. If they work in other departments, ask payroll or that department's manager for their hours this week before you call.

## Can a Texas Hospital Require a PRN Nurse to Take a Shift?

Generally no. Texas [Health and Safety Code Chapter 258](https://law.justia.com/codes/texas/health-and-safety-code/title-4/subtitle-b/chapter-258/) bars a hospital from requiring a nurse to work extra hours or extra days beyond their schedule, and a nurse may refuse. This next part is our reading of the law, not a court ruling: a PRN nurse has no set schedule, so any shift you order them into is a day beyond it. Treat every PRN shift as an offer.

The chapter has narrow exceptions, such as a declared disaster or an emergency the hospital could not have foreseen. Even then, section 258.004(b) of the chapter says the hospital shall, "to the extent possible, make a good faith effort to meet the staffing need through voluntary overtime, including calling per diems and agency nurses, assigning floats, or requesting an additional day of work from off-duty employees." In plain terms, even in an exception the hospital is expected to try volunteers first, and PRN nurses are named as one of them.

Other states set their own rules. Our [state guide to mandatory nurse overtime](/blog/mandatory-nurse-overtime-by-state) covers them.

Recruiting, credential verification, orientation and paying for the hours actually worked stay with HR, your credentialing process and your payroll or time clock system.

## What to Do This Week

1. **List every PRN nurse you have today,** with the units each one is cleared for and the date of their last shift.
2. **Set a deadline for PRN dates** a few days before you build the next schedule, and send the request now.
3. **Write your call order** for an open shift: anyone who can work it at straight time, then anyone who would go into overtime, then an agency.
4. **Add a column for hours this week** to your PRN list, so you can see who is close to 40 before you call.
5. **Ask two former staff nurses** whether they would join the PRN pool, and start their credential checks if they say yes.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: How many PRN nurses does a 25-bed hospital need?**

There is no published benchmark for a hospital that size, so size the pool from your own records. Count the shifts you covered with overtime or an agency last quarter: your payroll report shows overtime hours by week, and your agency invoices show agency shifts. Note which days and shifts they fell on. You need enough PRN nurses, cleared for the right units, to offer those shifts, allowing for the ones who will say no.

**Q: Do PRN nurses need the same credentialing as staff nurses?**

Yes. A PRN nurse needs license verification, the certifications your unit requires and orientation to your units before a first shift. The New York State Nurses Association describes the ideal internal pool as staff whose credentials have been evaluated and who have been oriented and trained to the facility.

**Q: Can a PRN nurse turn down a shift?**

Yes. A PRN shift is an offer. In Texas, Health and Safety Code Chapter 258 bars a hospital from requiring a nurse to work extra hours or extra days beyond their schedule, and a nurse may refuse.

**Q: What is the difference between a PRN pool and a float pool?**

A float pool is usually staff nurses with regular hours who move between units. A PRN pool is nurses with no guaranteed hours who work when they choose to. At a small hospital, the PRN pool often does the job a float pool does at a larger one.
