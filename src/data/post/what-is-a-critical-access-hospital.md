A critical access hospital (CAH) is a CMS-designated rural hospital with 25 or fewer acute care beds, located at least 35 miles from the nearest hospital (or 15 miles by secondary road in mountainous terrain), and providing 24-hour emergency care every day of the year. CAHs receive cost-based Medicare reimbursement rather than the prospective payment rates that apply to standard acute care hospitals, a material financial advantage. As of 2024, approximately 1,350 CAHs operate in the United States, with 84 located in Texas, according to the [Flex Monitoring Team](https://www.flexmonitoring.org/critical-access-hospital-locations-list). The designation is governed by [42 CFR Part 485, Subpart F](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-42/chapter-IV/subchapter-G/part-485/subpart-F).

## Key Takeaways

- The defining limit is 25 or fewer acute care inpatient beds. Cross it and the hospital no longer qualifies as a critical access hospital.
- It must sit more than 35 miles from the nearest hospital, or more than 15 miles in mountainous terrain or where only secondary roads connect.
- It must keep a 96-hour annual average length of stay for acute care across the facility, not a cap on any single patient.
- Payment is cost-based, not the fixed prospective rates standard hospitals receive. That model is the financial reason rural hospitals fight to keep the designation.
- It must keep a registered nurse on duty or immediately on call at all times under 42 CFR Section 485.631.
- Texas has roughly 84 critical access hospitals, among the states with the highest counts.

## How Does a Critical Access Hospital Work?

CMS established the CAH designation through the Balanced Budget Act of 1997 in response to a wave of rural hospital closures in the 1980s and early 1990s. To qualify, a hospital must meet all of the following criteria:

- **Location:** Rural area, at least 35 miles from the nearest hospital (or 15 miles in mountainous terrain or where only secondary roads exist)
- **Bed limit:** No more than 25 acute care inpatient beds (swing beds for skilled nursing are counted separately)
- **Length of stay:** Average annual inpatient stay of 96 hours or less
- **Emergency care:** 24-hour emergency services, 365 days per year
- **State designation:** Must be designated by the state as a necessary provider

The financial benefit of CAH status is substantial. While standard hospitals are reimbursed at fixed prospective payment rates (often below actual cost), CAHs receive [101% of their reasonable Medicare costs](https://www.cms.gov/medicare/health-safety-standards/certification-compliance/critical-access-hospitals). This cost-based reimbursement model is the primary reason rural hospitals actively pursue and protect their CAH designation.

Staffing requirements are codified at [42 CFR Section 485.631](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-42/chapter-IV/subchapter-G/part-485/subpart-F/section-485.631). CAHs must have a registered nurse, clinical nurse specialist, or licensed practical nurse on duty whenever the hospital has one or more inpatients, and a registered nurse must be on duty or on call to furnish patient care at all times. Compliance with this requirement must be documented and surveyable, which has direct implications for how nurse scheduling is structured and recorded.

The Medicare Rural Hospital Flexibility (Flex) Program, run by [HRSA](https://www.hrsa.gov/rural-health), provides technical assistance and grant funding to CAHs in most states. In Texas, [TORCH (Texas Organization of Rural and Community Hospitals)](https://www.torchnet.org) supports CAHs with compliance guidance, workforce resources, and advocacy.

**Reimbursement model:** Community hospitals receive fixed Medicare Prospective Payment System (PPS) rates; CAHs receive cost-based reimbursement at 101% of reasonable costs. Cost management is rewarded rather than penalized, and unnecessary staffing cost hits the margin directly.

**Bed limit:** CAHs are capped at 25 acute care beds by federal regulation. Standard community hospitals have no such federal cap; state certificate-of-need laws may apply separately.

**Location requirement:** CAHs must be rural and geographically isolated. Community hospitals can be urban, suburban, or rural without restriction.

**Staffing requirements:** The [Section 485.631](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-42/chapter-IV/subchapter-G/part-485/subpart-F/section-485.631) requirement for continuous RN availability is CAH-specific. Standard hospitals are governed by CMS Conditions of Participation at 42 CFR Part 482, which have different nurse staffing language. Texas also applies its own nursing facility regulations, but CAH-specific federal requirements add a compliance layer that community hospitals do not face.

**Administrative capacity:** Most CAHs operate with lean administrative staff. The nurse manager at a 20-bed CAH often handles scheduling, compliance documentation, and direct patient care simultaneously, a workload that would be distributed across multiple roles at a larger facility.

## Why Does Critical Access Hospital Status Matter for Nurse Managers?

For nurse managers at critical access hospitals, the CAH designation is not an abstract regulatory category, it shapes daily operations.

Under [Section 485.631](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-42/chapter-IV/subchapter-G/part-485/subpart-F/section-485.631), a gap in nurse coverage is a compliance violation, not just an inconvenience. Every shift must be covered by credentialed staff and documented, because during a CMS survey the scheduling record is the evidence. That is why nurse managers work to [stay CMS compliant with nurse scheduling](/blog/how-to-stay-cms-compliant-nurse-scheduling).

Because reimbursement is cost-based, unnecessary overtime shows up directly in the hospital's Medicare cost reports. Trimming it improves reimbursement sustainability, not just the monthly budget. In Texas, this overlaps with [Texas nursing overtime compliance](/blog/texas-nursing-overtime-compliance-cah) under both federal and state wage rules.

The small team size at most CAHs means there is little margin for error. With 10-20 nurses on staff, a single unplanned absence can require a full schedule rebuild. Per diem pools, [after-hours callout coverage](/blog/after-hours-callout-coverage-small-hospitals), and documented on-call availability are operational necessities, not optional enhancements. Most CAHs also handle this with [no IT department](/blog/nurse-scheduling-no-it-department-rural-hospital) to lean on, which is why purpose-built [scheduling software for a 25-bed hospital](/blog/best-scheduling-software-25-bed-hospital) matters more here than at a large facility.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: What qualifies a hospital as a critical access hospital?**
A hospital must be located in a rural area at least 35 miles from the nearest hospital, maintain 25 or fewer acute care beds, provide 24/7 emergency care, keep average inpatient stays under 96 hours, and receive state designation as a necessary provider. CMS administers the federal designation under 42 CFR Part 485, Subpart F.

**Q: How many critical access hospitals are in Texas?**
As of 2024, Texas has roughly 84 critical access hospitals, among the states with the highest counts, according to the [Flex Monitoring Team](https://www.flexmonitoring.org/critical-access-hospital-locations-list). They are concentrated in West Texas, the Panhandle, and rural East Texas. [TORCH (Texas Organization of Rural and Community Hospitals)](https://www.torchnet.org) maintains a current list and supports the state's CAHs with compliance and workforce resources.

**Q: What are the staffing requirements for critical access hospitals?**
Under [42 CFR Section 485.631](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-42/chapter-IV/subchapter-G/part-485/subpart-F/section-485.631), CAHs must have a registered nurse on duty or immediately on call at all times, with at least one nurse on duty whenever there is an inpatient. The CAH must document compliance. State nursing regulations layer on top, and Texas-specific rules from [HHSC](https://www.hhs.texas.gov/providers/health-care-facilities-regulation/hospitals-general-hospitals) apply alongside the CMS conditions of participation.

**Q: What is the difference between a hospital and a critical access hospital?**
A critical access hospital is capped at 25 acute care beds, must sit in a rural area more than 35 miles from the nearest hospital, and receives cost-based Medicare reimbursement at 101% of reasonable costs. Standard hospitals have no federal bed cap and are paid fixed prospective payment rates. CAHs also carry the [Section 485.631](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-42/chapter-IV/subchapter-G/part-485/subpart-F/section-485.631) always-on-duty nurse rule.

**Q: Can a critical access hospital have an ICU?**
A critical access hospital can operate intensive or critical care services, but those beds count toward the 25-bed acute care limit. Many CAHs run a small number of monitored beds rather than a dedicated ICU, and stabilize then transfer the most complex critical care patients to larger facilities. Service scope is set under the [CMS CAH conditions of participation](https://www.cms.gov/medicare/health-safety-standards/certification-compliance/critical-access-hospitals).

**Q: How long can a patient stay in a critical access hospital?**
There is no cap on how long an individual patient can stay. The 96-hour figure is an annual average length of stay for acute care patients across the whole facility, not a limit on any single admission. A CAH must maintain that annual average to keep its designation, per [CMS](https://www.cms.gov/medicare/health-safety-standards/certification-compliance/critical-access-hospitals) and [RuralHealthInfo](https://www.ruralhealthinfo.org/topics/critical-access-hospitals).
