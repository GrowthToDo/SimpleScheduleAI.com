- **Three Texas state directed payment programs missed federal approval** for the state fiscal year that began **September 1, 2026**. The largest program, the Comprehensive Hospital Increase Reimbursement Program (CHIRP), is more than **$9.1 billion** of a withheld total above **$9.8 billion** ([HFMA, September 15, 2026](https://www.hfma.org/payment-reimbursement-and-managed-care/texas-medicaid-hospital-funding/))

Texas hospitals spent seventeen days without a Medicaid payment stream worth billions a year, counting September 1 through September 17, 2026 inclusive. If you run a Critical Access Hospital in Texas, the question the reporting never answered is whether yours was in the group that lost it. The Texas rule that decides who CHIRP pays names the Critical Access Hospital designation directly.

## What Texas Medicaid Payments Stopped on September 1, 2026?

Three Texas state directed payment programs did not get federal approval in time for the state fiscal year that began September 1, 2026. These programs raise what Medicaid managed care organizations pay certain providers.

The largest is CHIRP, at [more than $9.1 billion of the more than $9.8 billion held up](https://www.hfma.org/payment-reimbursement-and-managed-care/texas-medicaid-hospital-funding/). HFMA describes the other two as covering medical groups and rural health clinics. Texas calls these programs directed payment programs, or DPPs, and the two alongside CHIRP are TIPPS, the Texas Incentives for Physicians and Professional Services, and RAPPS, the Rural Access to Primary and Preventive Services.

The money is not raised by the state. Local entities levy an assessment on hospitals and transfer it to the state, which draws federal matching funds against it. These are local assessments rather than state taxes, and that distinction is what the CMS objection turns on.

In a letter dated September 3, 2026, CMS raised two objections. It wanted the provider tax tests applied separately to each class of service, where Texas applies them to aggregate hospital net patient revenue. It also questioned how local governments are grouped into participation districts.

Four numbers are circulating and they measure different things.

| Figure                 | What it measures                                                             | Source                                                         |
| ---------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| More than $9.8 billion | Withheld across all three programs for the 2027 state fiscal year            | HFMA, September 15, 2026                                       |
| More than $9.1 billion | The hospital program's share of that total                                   | HFMA, September 15, 2026                                       |
| $27 million a day      | Estimated statewide impact on hospitals, an estimate and not a measured loss | Texas Hospital Association, via Texas Tribune, August 31, 2026 |
| Nearly $12 billion     | What the September 17 approval authorizes, across five provider categories   | Governor's office, via Texas Tribune, September 17, 2026       |

The last row covers five provider categories and the first covers three programs, which is most of the gap. Texas runs five directed payment programs. The two that pay nursing facilities and behavioral health providers are not among the three named here, and they were not among the three withheld in the 2021 hold described below either. Current per program amounts are not published, so the dollars do not reconcile exactly.

Seventeen days at that estimate is roughly $459 million, our own math on a statewide number rather than a per hospital figure.

## Were Critical Access Hospitals Inside CHIRP When the Payments Stopped?

Yes, by definition. The eligible class CHIRP pays names the designation directly, through three provisions of the Texas Administrative Code. Whether a given hospital was enrolled is a separate question, below.

| Rule                                                                                                | What it says                                                                                                                 | Why it matters                  |
| --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------- |
| [1 TAC 353.1306(d)(1)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/texas/1-Tex-Admin-Code-SS-353.1306)  | Lists six hospital classes CHIRP directs payments to. The second is rural hospitals                                          | A rural class sits inside CHIRP |
| [1 TAC 353.1306(b)(9)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/texas/1-Tex-Admin-Code-SS-353.1306)  | Defines that class only by pointing elsewhere, to "a hospital that is a rural hospital as defined in 355.8052 of this title" | The definition lives elsewhere  |
| [1 TAC 355.8052(b)(35)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/regulations/texas/1-Tex-Admin-Code-SS-355-8052) | Defines a rural hospital by three tests, one of which is a hospital "designated by Medicare as a Critical Access Hospital"   | Names the designation           |

That last rule sets three tests. A hospital in a county of 68,750 or fewer people by the 2020 census. A hospital carrying one of three Medicare designations, Critical Access Hospital, Sole Community Hospital or Rural Referral Center, outside a metropolitan area. Or a hospital of 100 or fewer beds holding one of those three designations inside one.

There is participation evidence too, though older. A [Texas Hospital Association whitepaper](https://www.tha.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/01/2022-Medicaid-DPP-Whitepaper.pdf) covering 394 CHIRP participants includes a hospital type line reading "Critical Access Hospital (77)." It states that without these programs, "baseline negative adjusted margins in small, rural, and critical access hospitals would fall 1-2%."

Two caveats. That count describes 2022, not today. And eligibility is not automatic, because CHIRP raises what managed care organizations pay, so a hospital has to be enrolled and [in network with the plan](https://www.superiorhealthplan.com/content/dam/centene/Superior/Provider/PDFs/dpp-faq-508.pdf).

## Does Medicare Cost Based Reimbursement Protect a Hospital From a Medicaid Freeze?

No. Cost based payment is a Medicare arrangement and it does not touch Medicaid.

A Critical Access Hospital is [reimbursed by Medicare at allowable cost plus one percent](https://www.ruralhealthinfo.org/topics/critical-access-hospitals), subject to the two percent sequestration reduction in place since April 2013. Each state decides separately how it pays these hospitals through Medicaid, and Texas pays them partly through the programs above.

[Texas has 93 critical access hospitals](https://www.ruralhealthinfo.org/states/texas), per federal data current to April 2026. That Medicare arrangement is the only payment the designation itself guarantees.

One assumption is worth correcting. The measure is days cash on hand, and on it the smallest hospitals do best. Nationally, [Chartis puts the median](https://www.chartis.com/insights/2026-rural-health-state-state) at 29 days for rural and community hospitals. Critical Access Hospitals hold 98. Medicaid is close to ten percent of net revenue at a typical rural hospital, a national figure rather than a Texas one. A hospital in that position absorbs a seventeen day gap out of reserves. A seven month one is a different question.

## Did Any Texas Medicaid Freeze Coverage Name a Critical Access Hospital?

One hospital was named, and never identified as a Critical Access Hospital. The Bond Buyer reported a credit downgrade at Kimble County Hospital District, which runs a 15 bed Critical Access Hospital in Junction, designated in 2000. No other outlet we read named a hospital of that size.

We read the freeze window coverage across the outlets listed in Sources: two Texas Tribune reports, HFMA, KTRE and The Bond Buyer. Several of them carried the same wire copy.

One report broke that pattern. [The Bond Buyer, on September 8, 2026](https://www.bondbuyer.com/news/texas-hospitals-face-financial-headwinds), reported a Moody's one notch downgrade of Kimble County Hospital District to Baa3, citing its "very small operating scale, with combined operating revenue of approximately $13.5 million." Kimble Hospital in Junction is a 15 bed Critical Access Hospital, designated in 2000, and it appears on [our own list of the state's critical access hospitals](/blog/critical-access-hospitals-in-texas).

That article never uses the phrase Critical Access Hospital, and never connects the downgrade to the freeze. A hospital of exactly this size did surface, as a credit rating line, with the question of whether CHIRP paid it left for the reader to ask.

We found no statement from the Texas Organization of Rural and Community Hospitals on either the freeze or the restoration. Its chief executive, John Henderson, was quoted in The Bond Buyer inside the window, but on a separate report about rural closure risk rather than on the withheld payments. The organization publishes no dated news archive, so that is a gap in the record rather than a confirmed silence. Everything else was conditional, in the shape of "would be forced to" and "if these delays continue."

The closest rural specific comment on the freeze came from John Hawkins, President and Chief Executive Officer of the Texas Hospital Association, [speaking to KTRE](https://www.ktre.com/2026/09/05/texas-hospitals-face-27m-daily-loss-federal-medicaid-funds-withheld/).

> "We're especially concerned about rural hospitals, the thin margins that they're operating with, the fact that they usually have a higher Medicaid population than a lot of the other hospitals in the state."

Hawkins was describing a risk rather than reporting an event.

## Has Texas Medicaid Directed Payment Funding Been Withheld Before?

Yes, and the parallel is close. The Texas Hospital Association's own whitepaper records it. It says CMS "withheld approval of three DPPs, CHIRP, TIPPS, and RAPPS, from September 2021 through March 2022 due to a dispute over Texas' method of financing the nonfederal share of payments."

Same three programs, same question about financing the nonfederal share. That hold ran about seven months, against seventeen days in 2026.

The number that moved is the daily one. During the 2021 delay, by the association's account, "hospitals lost more than $7 million per day." The 2026 estimate is $27 million a day. The same dispute over the same programs now carries close to four times the daily exposure, a comparison absent from the coverage.

The same document warns that "any future interruption, disallowance, or disapproval of DPPs, similar to the lapse that occurred in SFY 2022, would prove destabilizing."

## What Is Still Unknown About the Restored Texas Medicaid Payments?

Three things. Whether the seventeen day gap is paid retroactively, whether the approval carries conditions Texas had to accept, and how long the claims backlog takes to clear. None has been stated publicly.

No CMS approval letter has been made public, so nobody outside the negotiation knows whether Texas agreed to change anything. On retroactivity, no state or federal statement we found addresses whether the seventeen day gap is paid back or written off.

On timing, [Anna Stelter, Vice President of Policy at the Texas Hospital Association](https://www.texastribune.org/2026/08/31/texas-hospitals-medicaid-funding-chirp/), spoke before the approval. She said: "Even if we get an approval soon, there will be a claims backlog that takes at least 90 days to clear. And the bigger the claims backlog, the longer it takes to catch up."

These programs are approved annually, so September 2026 did not permanently settle the disagreement underneath this one.

## What to Do This Week

1. **Confirm your enrollment, not your eligibility.** Ask your finance lead whether this hospital is enrolled in CHIRP and in which class. The rule already makes the designation qualifying, so eligibility is not the open question. Enrollment and network status are.
2. **Ask what the September payment actually was.** If enrolled, find out whether the gap is treated as delayed or as lost. Those are different numbers on a cash forecast.
3. **Write down anything that was paused.** Hiring, agency use, overtime approval, a capital item. Nothing in the coverage we read documented what a hospital actually paused, so your record may be the only one you get.
4. **Pull your last eight weeks of overtime by nurse.** If a cost conversation is coming, that table is its input. Gathering it under time pressure is how the wrong shift gets cut.
5. **Put a reminder in the calendar for next August.** These approvals run a year at a time and the state fiscal year starts September 1.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: Does a Critical Access Hospital qualify for CHIRP?**

CHIRP is the Comprehensive Hospital Increase Reimbursement Program, the largest of the three withheld. It pays a rural hospital class, and Texas rule 1 TAC 355.8052(b)(35) defines a rural hospital to include a hospital Medicare designates as a Critical Access Hospital. Qualifying is not the same as being enrolled, so confirm your own status with your finance lead.

**Q: Why did Medicaid funding stop in Texas on September 1, 2026?**

Federal approval of three state directed payment programs did not arrive before the state fiscal year began. CMS objected to how Texas applies provider tax tests, and to how local governments are grouped into participation districts.

**Q: Has this happened to Texas before?**

Yes. CMS withheld the same three programs from September 2021 through March 2022 over the same financing question. Hospitals lost more than $7 million a day then, against an estimated $27 million a day in 2026.

**Q: Will hospitals be paid for the days the funding was stopped?**

That has not been reported. Coverage at the time of the approval noted it was not clear when funds would be transferred. The Texas Hospital Association had said a claims backlog would take at least ninety days to clear.
