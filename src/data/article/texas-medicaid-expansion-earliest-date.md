## Key Takeaways

- An expansion would happen in two steps. First a Texas law takes effect. Then, after federal approval and setup, newly covered patients start arriving at your hospital.
- The Texas Legislature meets on January 12, 2027. If an ordinary bill passes and the session runs its full 140 days, the [Texas Constitution](https://texaslegalguide.com/Texas_Constitution:Article_III,_Section_39) puts the law's start no earlier than August 30, 2027, by our count.
- A law can take effect the day the governor signs it only if two-thirds of all members of the House and two-thirds of all members of the Senate vote for that.
- Coverage would come months after the law. North Carolina took 249 days from signing its law to covering new patients, and South Dakota took 235 days from its vote. Taken as an illustration, those timelines would put Texas coverage between November 2027 and May 2028.
- Texas would also have to set up a work-hour check that neither North Carolina nor South Dakota had to build. Under the [2025 federal budget law](https://www.congress.gov/119/plaws/publ21/PLAW-119publ21.htm), most adults who get Medicaid through an expansion must meet a work-hour rule. Each month, those adults must work, volunteer or join a work program for at least 80 hours, or be in school at least half-time. The state checks this when they apply and at each renewal.

Texas Medicaid expansion means covering adults aged 19 to 64 with incomes up to 138% of the federal poverty level. Today Texas Medicaid covers parents only up to 15% of the poverty level, [KFF reports](https://www.kff.org/medicaid/issue-brief/how-many-uninsured-are-in-the-coverage-gap-and-how-many-could-be-eligible-if-all-states-adopted-the-medicaid-expansion/). On an ordinary vote and a full-length session, a Texas expansion law could not take effect before August 30, 2027. Newly covered patients would arrive later still, after federal approval and setup. This article lays out each step and the date the rules allow.

## What Did Texas's Governor-Elect Say About Medicaid Expansion?

She said she wants it. "The way we do that is expanding Medicaid and that's my position," Governor-elect Gina Hinojosa told the [Texas Tribune](https://www.texastribune.org/2026/11/09/texas-medicaid-expansion-gina-hinojosa/) in a story published November 9, 2026. The Tribune reports that expansion is a top priority for her and for Lieutenant Governor-elect Vikki Goodwin.

The Tribune's headline says an executive order will not make expansion happen. The paper reports that "There is a Texas law that prevents the state from altering the eligibility requirements of Medicaid without legislative approval." It adds that it is "not exactly clear" whether that law stops a governor from directing the state's health agency to send an expansion plan to Washington.

The Legislature will be split. Democrats will control the Texas House next year, and Republicans will still control the Texas Senate, according to the Tribune. The Tribune also reports one idea discussed in the week after the election: a "trigger" bill. That bill would turn expansion on only if Congress first repeals parts of the 2025 federal budget law.

## What Is the Earliest Date a Texas Medicaid Expansion Law Could Take Effect?

August 30, 2027, on an ordinary vote, if the 2027 session runs its full 140 days as the 2025 session did. A session that ends early would bring the date forward, and a special session would push it later. The table shows each step. The rules come from the Texas Constitution, Texas law and federal regulations, and the dates in the last column are our own count.

## How Long Did Other States Take From an Expansion Law to Coverage?

About eight months, in the two most recent examples. We counted the days between the dates each state published.

- **North Carolina: 249 days.** Gov. Roy Cooper [signed the expansion law on March 27, 2023](https://governor.nc.gov/news/press-releases/2023/03/27/governor-cooper-signs-medicaid-expansion-law), and [coverage started on December 1, 2023](https://www.ncdhhs.gov/news/press-releases/2023/09/25/nc-medicaid-expansion-will-launch-dec-1-2023). Part of that wait was a stalled state budget. The governor said on Friday, September 22, 2023 that he would let the budget become law, and coverage started 70 days later.
- **South Dakota: 235 days.** [Voters approved a constitutional amendment on November 8, 2022](https://ballotpedia.org/South_Dakota_Medicaid_Expansion_Amendment_Initiative_%282022%29), and the amendment itself set July 1, 2023 as the start date. Coverage started that day, according to [KFF's tracker of state expansion decisions](https://www.kff.org/affordable-care-act/state-indicator/state-activity-around-expanding-medicaid-under-the-affordable-care-act/).

Applied to Texas, as an illustration only. Each row starts from the August 30, 2027 date in the table above.

| What we measured                                              | Days | If Texas took as long after August 30, 2027 |

## What Federal Rules Would Come With a Texas Medicaid Expansion Now?

A state that expands now takes on rules from the [2025 federal budget law](https://www.congress.gov/119/plaws/publ21/PLAW-119publ21.htm) (Public Law 119-21) that earlier expansions did not have. These rules apply only to adults who would get Medicaid through the expansion: adults 19 to 64 who qualify only because Texas expanded. Texas does not cover that group today, so most of these rules would not touch current Texas Medicaid patients. The exception is the rule on care paid before an application, which changes for almost everyone in 2027.

- **Work-hour rule.** Most adults covered through the expansion must work, volunteer or join a work program for at least 80 hours a month, or be in school at least half-time. Earning at least $580 a month, which is 80 hours at the $7.25 federal minimum wage, also counts. Some adults are exempt, such as parents of children 13 and under, and adults the state finds medically frail, such as those with a disability or a serious medical condition. The state checks the hours when a person applies and at each renewal, and may check more often. [CMS says states must generally start the rule by January 1, 2027](https://www.cms.gov/newsroom/fact-sheets/medicaid-community-engagement-requirement-certain-individuals-interim-final-rule-comment-period-cms).
- **Work hours before coverage starts.** A new applicant must also show the 80 hours for one to three months before the month they apply. The state picks the number of months.
- **Renewals every six months.** Each adult covered through the expansion has their eligibility checked every six months, instead of once a year.
- **Less care paid before the application.** Today Medicaid can pay for care in the [three months before someone applies](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/42/1396a). For applications from January 1, 2027, the 2025 law cuts that to two months for most people. Adults covered through the expansion get one month. The two-month change applies in Texas whether or not Texas expands.
- **No extra federal bonus.** The federal government still pays [90% of the cost of covering adults through the expansion](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/42/1396d), and Texas would pay the other 10%. States that expanded late used to get an extra 5 percentage points of federal funding on their other Medicaid costs for two years. That bonus now goes only to states that started before January 1, 2026.
- **Lower limits on two hospital funding tools.** Provider taxes are fees a state charges hospitals, which the state can put toward its share of Medicaid. State-directed payments are extra Medicaid payments a state tells its Medicaid health plans to pay hospitals. For a tax a state already had in July 2025, an expansion state's limit drops in steps, from 5.5% in federal fiscal year 2028 to 3.5% from fiscal year 2032. The limit never rises above the state's existing level. States that have not expanded stay at their existing level. It also caps state-directed payments at 100% of what Medicare pays, against 110% for states that did not expand.

The last item matters to Texas hospitals. CHIRP is a Texas program of extra Medicaid payments to hospitals, and its rules name critical access hospitals, as our [article on the Texas Medicaid payment freeze](/articles/texas-medicaid-freeze-critical-access-hospitals) explains. CHIRP is a state-directed payment program. The law applies the 100% limit to states that start expanding after July 4, 2025. CHIRP is a program Texas already had, and we found no source on how the new limit would apply to it, or from when. Your hospital association should ask the state health agency and CMS before anyone counts on a CHIRP figure.

## What Does Medicaid Expansion Mean for Rural Hospital Finances?

Rural hospitals in states that expanded have been in better financial shape on average, but the studies show a link, not proof that expansion caused it. Both main sources also describe expansions that came before the 2025 rules.

- **Chartis, February 2026.** [52.2% of rural hospitals in states that did not expand were losing money, against 34.9% in states that did](https://www.chartis.com/insights/2026-rural-health-state-state). Texas is one of the states that did not expand. Our article on [rural hospital losses and nurse staffing costs](/articles/rural-hospital-losses-nurse-staffing-cost) covers that report.
- **Health Affairs, 2018.** A study of 2008 to 2016 data [found expansion "associated with improved hospital financial performance and substantially lower likelihoods of closure, especially in rural markets"](https://doi.org/10.1377/hlthaff.2017.0976).

The number of people involved is large. About 1.1 million uninsured Texas adults would become eligible, [KFF estimates from 2024 data](https://www.kff.org/medicaid/issue-brief/how-many-uninsured-are-in-the-coverage-gap-and-how-many-could-be-eligible-if-all-states-adopted-the-medicaid-expansion/). These uninsured adults are the patients who could come to your emergency room with Medicaid instead of no coverage.

Neither the Chartis report nor the Health Affairs study measured an expansion with a work-hour rule, six-month renewals and one month of back payment. We found no study of how those rules, taken together, change hospital finances, so we cannot put a number on it.

The back-payment rules reach the business office directly. Take a patient admitted on March 5 who applies for Medicaid on May 10. Under today's three-month rule, coverage would reach back to February 1. Under the two-month rule for most applicants from 2027, it would reach back to March 1. Both of those dates include the March stay. Under the one-month rule for adults covered through the expansion, coverage reaches back only to April 1, so Medicaid would not pay for the March stay.

## What to Do This Week

1. **Size your uninsured adult patients.** Ask your business office for last year's count of inpatient and emergency visits by uninsured patients aged 19 to 64. Expansion covers only the low-income adults in that age group, so treat the count as an upper limit. It tells your leadership team how much of your uninsured care an expansion could touch.
2. **Time your Medicaid applications.** Ask whoever helps uninsured patients apply for Medicaid, usually patient access or a financial counselor, how many days pass between a visit and the application. From January 1, 2027, most Medicaid applicants in Texas will be covered only from the second month before they apply, expansion or not. A patient seen in March who applies in June would not be covered for the March visit.
3. **Send your hospital association one question in writing.** If Texas expanded, how would the 100% limit on state-directed payments for expansion states apply to CHIRP, and from what date?
4. **Keep expansion money out of your 2027 budget.** On an ordinary vote and a full-length session, the law could not take effect before August 30, 2027. Budget on today's mix of insured and uninsured patients, and add expansion only once a law passes.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: Can Texas's governor expand Medicaid by executive order?**

It is not clear. The Texas Tribune reports that a Texas law bars changing who qualifies for Medicaid without the Legislature's approval. The paper also says it is "not exactly clear" whether that law stops a governor from directing the state's health agency to send an expansion plan to Washington.

**Q: When is the earliest a Texas Medicaid expansion law could take effect?**

On an ordinary vote, August 30, 2027, the 91st day after the 2027 session ends on May 31, if the session runs its full 140 days. With a two-thirds vote of all members in both chambers, it could take effect the day the governor signs it. Coverage would start later, after federal approval and setup.

**Q: Would the new federal work rules apply to people already on Texas Medicaid?**

No. The work-hour rule applies only to adults who get Medicaid through an expansion: adults 19 to 64 who qualify only because a state expanded. Texas does not cover that group today. One other part of the 2025 law does reach current Texas Medicaid applicants: from January 1, 2027, most will be covered only from the second month before they apply, instead of the third.
