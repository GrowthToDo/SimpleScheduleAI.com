## Key Takeaways

- On November 5, 2026, CMS proposed a rule, [CMS-3484-P](https://public-inspection.federalregister.gov/2026-22812.pdf), that would remove the annual emergency readiness training required of every critical access hospital since July 1, 2025.
- It is a proposal. The training rule stays in force until a final rule takes effect, and CMS itself says the final rule is unlikely before January 1, 2027. Keep training your staff.
- The emergency protocols themselves stay. Surveyors can still ask a nurse to "demonstrate or explain" a protocol under survey tag C-0896.
- CMS assumes every medical staff member at a critical access hospital takes this training, against one in five at other hospitals, because small-hospital staff cover more than one unit.
- Because of that assumption, critical access hospitals would get about 17 percent of the savings, even though they have only 2.7 percent of hospital beds. For an average critical access hospital, by our division of CMS's totals, the training takes about 73 staff hours a year, worth about $8,300. The table below shows every step.
- Comments are due 60 days after the rule is published in the Federal Register. Publication is scheduled for November 10, which would put the deadline at January 9, 2027.

CMS has proposed removing the annual emergency readiness training that every critical access hospital has owed its staff since July 1, 2025. Nothing changes yet. The proposal is one part of a large rule CMS calls CARE, short for "Cutting Administrative Requirements for Excellence in Patient Care." The training rule still applies until a final rule takes effect. For a nurse manager, the useful parts are what stays, what CMS's own numbers say about hospitals your size, and the comment deadline.

## What Did CMS Propose for Critical Access Hospitals?

CMS proposed removing one paragraph of the emergency services standard: [42 CFR 485.618(e)(2)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/42/485.618), the staff training requirement. That paragraph says "Applicable staff, as identified by the CAH, must be trained annually on the protocols and provisions" a critical access hospital has for emergencies, including obstetric emergencies. The proposal is in rule CMS-3484-P, issued November 5, 2026 and scheduled for the Federal Register on November 10.

Removing the paragraph would also remove the paperwork that comes with it. Today the hospital's governing body has to identify which staff must be trained, and the hospital has to record completed training in personnel files and show that staff know the material.

CMS says it changed its mind. In 2024 it rejected comments that the training duplicated other rules. Now it points to several existing safeguards: its other hospital rules, EMTALA (the federal law that requires emergency screening and stabilizing care), accreditation standards, board certification and state licensing. Together, CMS says, those give "a sufficient framework to ensure staff competency in emergency services readiness without the need for a separate, prescriptive annual training requirement."

The protocols stay. The same standard still requires "adequate provisions and protocols to meet the emergency needs of patients," following national guidelines, including for obstetric emergencies. Only the separate annual training requirement would go.

## Does the Emergency Training Rule Still Apply Right Now?

Yes. A proposed rule changes nothing on its own. The training requirement has been in force since July 1, 2025, and stays in force until a final rule takes effect.

CMS also says when to expect that final rule. In the proposal it writes that "it is unlikely that this proposed rule will be finalized before January 1, 2027." So plan on the emergency training rule still applying on that date.

CMS made one request to surveyors, and it covers only the obstetric items due January 1, 2027. For surveys after that date, CMS asks surveyors to "focus on those CoPs that are not potentially subject to imminent revision." CoPs are the Conditions of Participation, the federal rules a hospital must meet to bill Medicare. The proposal makes no such request for the emergency training rule, which has been in force since July 2025.

Training also shows up in a survey whether or not the rule changes. Under tag C-0896, which CMS added in September, surveyors can ask staff to "demonstrate or explain" an emergency protocol step by step. A nurse who has never been taught the postpartum hemorrhage protocol will struggle with that question either way. Our [guide to tag C-0896](/articles/cms-c-0896-emergency-protocols-critical-access-hospitals) covers what surveyors check.

## Why Is Emergency Readiness Training a Bigger Burden for Small Hospitals?

CMS assumes every medical staff member at a critical access hospital takes the training. At other hospitals it assumes one in five does. Its reason: "In hospitals, which have a larger number of beds, there is likely to be a greater division of staff among units, with medical staff specifically designated to work in emergency departments. In contrast, CAHs, which are smaller in size, are likely to have medical staff that work across units."

The table below walks through CMS's savings estimate one step at a time. The figures come from [Table 48 of the proposed rule](https://public-inspection.federalregister.gov/2026-22812.pdf). Each row shows how it is worked out from the rows above it.

CMS's method has three inputs. Every staff member who takes the training needs a 1-hour refresher each year, a new hire needs the 3-hour first training instead, and 21 percent of staff are new each year. CMS takes the 21 percent from the annual hospital turnover rate in a 2024 staffing report from Nursing Solutions Inc.

| Step                                   | How it is worked out     | Critical access hospitals | Other hospitals with emergency services |
| -------------------------------------- | ------------------------ | ------------------------- | --------------------------------------- |
| A. Number of hospitals                 | From CMS                 | 1,329                     | 3,319                                   |
| B. Medical staff in those hospitals    | From CMS                 | 68,234                    | 1,667,532                               |
| C. Share CMS assumes take the training | From CMS                 | 100 percent               | 20 percent                              |
| D. Staff who take the training         | B x C                    | 68,234                    | 333,506                                 |
| E. Hours for new staff                 | D x 21 percent x 3 hours | 42,987                    | 210,109                                 |
| F. Hours for returning staff           | D x 79 percent x 1 hour  | 53,905                    | 263,470                                 |
| G. Staff hours saved each year         | E + F                    | 96,892                    | 473,579                                 |
| H. Dollars saved each year             | G x about $114 an hour   | $11,061,162               | $54,063,623                             |
| I. Share of all the savings            | H / $65,124,785 total    | 17 percent                | 83 percent                              |
| J. Staff hours for an average hospital | G / A                    | 73 hours                  | 143 hours                               |
| K. Dollars for an average hospital     | H / A                    | $8,323                    | $16,289                                 |

Rows A, B, C, E, F, G and H are CMS's figures. Rows D, I, J and K are our division of them, and so is the $114 an hour in row H: CMS's dollars divided by its hours. CMS builds that hourly figure from wages plus overhead.

Row I is the point. Critical access hospitals have only 2.7 percent of the certified beds in CMS's count, meaning the beds Medicare has approved, yet they would get 17 percent of the savings. Row C is the reason. If CMS had assumed one in five at critical access hospitals too, row D would be 13,647 staff, and their savings would drop to about $2.2 million, about 4 percent of a $56.3 million total.

In plain terms, CMS is saying in writing that at a small hospital the same people cover the emergency department and the other units. That is why CMS counts every staff member as needing the training. Here is our reading of that for the schedule. The nurse you put on the medical floor at night may be the first one to an emergency. Under [42 CFR 485.618(d)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/42/485.618), the on-call practitioner can generally be up to 30 minutes away, or 60 in the most remote areas. The night nurse's role stays the same whatever CMS decides about the training rule. Our guide to [night shift coverage at a small hospital](/blog/night-shift-nurse-schedule-coverage) covers how those nights are staffed.

## How Many Staff Hours Does Annual Emergency Readiness Training Take?

You can run CMS's method on your own roster: 1 hour for each returning staff member, and 3 hours for each new hire.

Here is a made-up example, not a real hospital. Say 40 of your staff take the training, and 8 of them joined this year, close to CMS's 21 percent.

- 32 returning staff x 1 hour = 32 hours
- 8 new staff x 3 hours = 24 hours
- Total: 56 staff hours a year

Those hours come out of the schedule. At 12-hour shifts, 56 hours is close to five shifts of nurse time a year, before you count the cover needed while nurses sit in training. Swap in your own headcount and new hires to get your number.

## What Else Does CMS-3484-P Change for Critical Access Hospitals?

The rule reaches critical access hospitals in several other places. Three matter most for nursing.

- **Obstetric staff training.** CMS proposes removing [42 CFR 485.649(c)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/42/485.649), the obstetric services staff training due January 1, 2027. CMS still says it "strongly encourage[s] CAHs to continue investing in these activities."
- **Obstetric quality improvement.** CMS proposes removing the maternal health items in the quality program at [42 CFR 485.641(d)(4) and (e)(2)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/42/485.641), also due January 1, 2027. A hospital that offers obstetric services would otherwise have to bring its obstetric leaders into the quality program, track obstetric outcomes, and run one obstetric improvement project a year. The rest of the quality program stays.
- **The distance rule for keeping critical access status.** A critical access hospital generally has to be more than a 35-mile drive from the nearest hospital. CMS proposes writing into the regulation that psychiatric, rehabilitation and long-term care hospitals do not count as that nearest hospital. It would add rural emergency hospitals to the same list of hospitals that do not count. Our [guide to the CMS location review](/articles/cms-cah-location-eligibility-review-2026) explains the test.

The rule also touches fire-safety code and emergency power updates, birth tissue policies, and an exemption for four facilities under a 2026 law. The full rule covers those items.

## How Do You Comment on CMS-3484-P?

Comments go to CMS on [regulations.gov, docket CMS-2026-3631](https://www.regulations.gov/docket/CMS-2026-3631). A docket is the public online file for one rule, and CMS asks you to refer to file code CMS-3484-P. CMS also accepts mail at the address in the rule. Comments are due 60 days after the rule is published in the Federal Register. Publication is scheduled for November 10, 2026, which would put the deadline at January 9, 2027. Check the date on the published version.

Anyone can comment, including a nurse manager. Agree inside your hospital whether a comment goes in under the CEO's name, the director of nursing's, or your own. A comment from a small hospital is most useful when it is specific. Say how many staff you train, how many hours it takes, and what the training has or has not changed on your floor. If your hospital wants to keep the rule, say why.

## What to Do This Week

1. **Keep the annual emergency training going.** The rule is still in force, and nothing in the proposal pauses it.
2. **Count your training hours** with the method above: returning staff at 1 hour, new hires at 3 hours.
3. **Check which nurses on your night schedule** have been trained on this year's emergency protocols, including the obstetric ones.
4. **Decide who will comment, and whether,** before January 9, 2027, and write down your hospital's numbers while they are fresh.
5. **If you offer obstetric services,** keep preparing for the January 1, 2027 obstetric training and quality items. Those items take effect on that date unless a final rule removes them first.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: Did CMS remove the critical access hospital emergency training requirement?**

Not yet. CMS proposed removing it on November 5, 2026, in rule CMS-3484-P. The requirement at 42 CFR 485.618(e)(2) stays in force until a final rule takes effect, which CMS says is unlikely before January 1, 2027.

**Q: Do critical access hospitals still need emergency protocols?**

Yes. The proposal removes only the separate annual training requirement. A critical access hospital must still have "adequate provisions and protocols to meet the emergency needs of patients," including for obstetric emergencies, and surveyors still check them under tag C-0896.

**Q: How much does CMS say the training costs critical access hospitals?**

CMS estimates that removing it would save critical access hospitals $11,061,162 and 96,892 staff hours a year, across 1,329 hospitals. That is about $8,300 and 73 hours for an average hospital, by our division of CMS's figures.

**Q: When is the comment deadline for CMS-3484-P?**

Comments are due 60 days after the rule is published in the Federal Register. Publication is scheduled for November 10, 2026, which would put the deadline at January 9, 2027. Confirm the date on the published version.
