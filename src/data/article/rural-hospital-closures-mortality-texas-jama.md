## Key Takeaways

- A study in [JAMA Network Open](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2026.36325), published September 28, 2026, looked at the 14 rural Texas hospitals that closed between 2007 and 2018. After the closures, the closure counties and the counties bordering them had **11.60 more deaths a year for every 100,000 people living there** than comparison counties with no closure nearby. The deaths counted were from heart attack, stroke, sepsis, and asthma or COPD (chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, a long-term lung disease).
- A second way of drawing the affected area gave almost the same number, **11.52 more deaths per 100,000 a year**. The authors put either gap at **5.4%** of the death rate those counties had before the closures.
- With the closure counties taken out, the bordering counties alone still had **10.46 more deaths per 100,000 a year** than the comparison counties. The second way of drawing the area did not hold up the same way: it fell to 9.54, which could be chance.
- In a county of 10,000 people, 11.60 per 100,000 works out to about **1.2 more deaths a year than a comparison county with no closure nearby** (11.60 x 10,000 / 100,000). That is our math, for illustration only.
- The study shows a link, not proof that the closures caused the deaths. Its data ends in 2019.
- Only 3 of the 14 were Critical Access Hospitals, going by the [UNC Sheps Center closures tracker](https://www.shepscenter.unc.edu/programs-projects/rural-health/rural-hospital-closures/). The smallest Texas counties were left out because they had too few deaths to report.

In the Texas counties a new study was able to measure, deaths from four emergencies were higher after a rural hospital nearby closed than in comparison counties. The gap showed up in the counties next door as well. The study, by Mark Poisler and Kritee Gujral, came out in JAMA Network Open on September 28, 2026. It leaves out the smallest Texas counties, and it gives no result for critical access hospitals on their own.

## What Did the New JAMA Study Find About Rural Hospital Closure Mortality in Texas?

It found more deaths from four time-sensitive conditions in counties affected by a closure than in comparison counties. The four are heart attack, stroke, sepsis, and asthma or COPD. The authors call them time-sensitive because they "have response windows measured in hours or minutes."

The researchers counted deaths from those four conditions in Texas counties from 2006 to 2019, using federal death records. They left out 2020 because of COVID. Then they compared how deaths changed in affected counties with how they changed in comparison counties, over three to five years before and after each closure. A study built this way is called a difference-in-differences study.

They drew the affected area two ways, to check that the answer did not depend on one choice. The border-county design (first row of the table) counts the county where the hospital closed plus every county bordering it. The service-area design (second row) uses the closed hospital's [hospital service area](https://www.dartmouthatlas.org/research-methods/) and counts every county in it. A hospital service area is a group of ZIP codes whose residents get most of their hospital care from the same hospitals.

| Design                                                           | Counties affected | Comparison counties | Extra deaths per 100,000 residents a year, compared with comparison counties | 95% confidence interval |
| ---------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------- | ------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------- |
| Border-county: the closure county plus every bordering county    | 71                | 104                 | 11.60                                                                        | 4.62 to 18.58           |
| Service-area: every county in the closed hospital's service area | 53                | 122                 | 11.52                                                                        | 0.46 to 22.58           |

The confidence interval is the range the true number most likely sits in. Both ranges stay above zero, so the difference is unlikely to be chance. The second range is wide, and its low end is close to zero, so that result is the less certain of the two.

The authors put either result at "a 5.4% increase compared with baseline mortality in affected counties." Baseline means the death rate those counties had before the closures. In their words, the gap "began immediately after closures and continued to progress over several years."

The authors also ran checks, and several came out weaker. Three matter for how much weight the main number can carry:

- **A second statistical method.** The authors re-ran the numbers with a second method meant to correct a known weakness of the first. For the border-county design it gave 12.89 more deaths per 100,000 a year, with a range from minus 0.39 to 26.17. On this check alone the difference could be chance. For the service-area design it gave 17.17, and that range stayed above zero.
- **Other lists of causes of death.** The authors re-ran both designs using five other lists of time-sensitive conditions, four taken from earlier studies and one the authors built by combining them. In the border-county design, three of the five still showed more deaths with ranges above zero. The service-area design held for only one of the five. The authors wrote that the border-county design held up better when other kinds of time-sensitive conditions were added.
- **The bordering counties on their own.** With the closure counties taken out, the border-county result held at 10.46. The service-area result did not. The section on neighboring counties below covers this.

Neither main number passes every check. The border-county result held up better on the other lists and on the bordering counties alone. The service-area result held up better on the second method.

## How Many More Deaths a Year Would 11.6 per 100,000 Mean in a Small Texas County?

The study reports its result as a rate per 100,000 residents, measured against comparison counties with no closure nearby. Here is what the main result, 11.60 per 100,000 a year, works out to for two county sizes. This is our math, for illustration. It is not a prediction for any one hospital or county.

- **A county of 10,000 people:** 11.60 x 10,000 / 100,000 = about **1.2 extra deaths a year**. Using the study's confidence interval of 4.62 to 18.58 instead, it is between about 0.5 and 1.9.
- **A county of 20,000 people:** 11.60 x 20,000 / 100,000 = about **2.3 extra deaths a year**, or between about 0.9 and 3.7 using the same interval.

In the border-county design, these figures apply to a county that had a closure in it or next to it. One or two deaths a year is small enough to get lost in a single county's normal ups and downs. A study needs many counties to see a change that size, and this one used 175 counties and 14 years of data.

## Why Did the JAMA Study Leave Out the Smallest Texas Counties?

Because the federal death records do not show small numbers. The CDC's public death database, CDC WONDER, hides a death count when it is under 10. The authors dropped a county if, in any year of the study, it had fewer than 10 deaths from the four conditions. That took out 79 of Texas's 254 counties and left 175.

The counties left out are far smaller than the ones kept. The 79 left out averaged 4,439 people. The 175 kept averaged 148,168. On the paper's county map, most of the left-out counties sit in West Texas and the Panhandle. None of the 14 closure counties was left out.

The study could not look at those 79 counties, so it has no finding for them either way. The authors say the left-out counties "were more rural and smaller ... which limits generalizability to the most remote communities."

## Are Deaths Higher in Counties Next to a Closed Rural Hospital?

In this study, yes. The authors re-ran the border-county design with the closure counties taken out, so only the neighbors were left. Those bordering counties still had 10.46 more deaths per 100,000 a year than the comparison counties, with a confidence interval of 2.81 to 18.11. The authors wrote that this means the findings "were not restricted to the closure county."

The service-area design did not hold up the same way. With the closure counties taken out, that result fell to 9.54 per 100,000 a year and no longer ruled out chance. The authors read this as the closure counties driving most of the result in that design.

## What Does the Texas Rural Hospital Closure Study Not Show?

The study cannot tell us whether the closures caused the deaths. What it shows is that deaths were higher in affected counties than in comparison counties. The authors could not "adjust for more granular patient-level or health system differences," so something else that changed in those counties at the same time could be part of the answer.

Here is what else it does not cover:

- **Nurse staffing.** The study does not measure nurses or staffing. It mentions clinicians leaving after closures only in passing, citing earlier work.
- **Critical Access Hospitals on their own.** It does not report a result for them separately. Eleven of the 14 closures were other kinds of hospital.
- **Why deaths were higher.** The authors say they "could not isolate the exact mechanisms" behind it. They list possible reasons, "such as transport delays, overcrowding at nearby hospitals, progressive deterioration of clinical infrastructure, and forgone care." The study did not measure them. The evidence for longer ambulance rides and crowded neighboring hospitals comes from earlier studies they cite. Those studies found closures "lengthened emergency medical services transport times." They also found closures sent patients "to open hospitals that face capacity strain."
- **Anything after 2019.** The data ends before COVID, and before this year's [Texas Medicaid payment freeze](/articles/texas-medicaid-freeze-critical-access-hospitals).

The authors also say their estimates "may be conservative or downward biased." One reason they give is that some comparison counties may have been affected too. That would happen if the effects reached farther than the borders the study drew, and it would make the measured gap smaller.

## What to Do This Week

1. **List the hospitals in your transfer and EMS radius.** Mark which ones are rural, and note which ones you send heart attack, stroke and sepsis patients to. Chartis, a health care analytics firm, counted 50 Texas rural hospitals as vulnerable to closure in its [2026 Rural Health State of the State](https://www.chartis.com/insights/2026-rural-health-state-state) but does not name them, so some hospitals on your list may be among them.
2. **Pull your emergency protocols for heart attack, stroke and sepsis,** three of the four conditions in this study. Surveyors now check emergency protocols under [CMS tag C-0896](/articles/cms-c-0896-emergency-protocols-critical-access-hospitals). The federal rule says "Applicable staff, as identified by the CAH, must be trained annually on the protocols" ([42 CFR 485.618(e)(2)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/42/485.618)). Check whose names are on that training list.
3. **Ask your EMS director how run times would change** if the nearest hospital in each direction closed. Note any route where the drive to the next hospital that takes stroke or heart attack patients gets longer. Share that list with your administrator and your medical staff.
4. **Look at your next four weeks of night and weekend schedules.** Write down how many nurses and which provider cover the emergency room each shift. Then ask your charge nurses what they would do if two extra ambulances arrived on one of those nights. Our guide to [night shift coverage at a small hospital](/blog/night-shift-nurse-schedule-coverage) covers how night coverage is set up.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: Did the rural hospital closures in Texas cause the deaths?**

The study cannot say that. It found more deaths in affected counties than in comparison counties, which is a link, not proof of cause. The authors say they could not isolate why.

**Q: Were any of the closed Texas hospitals Critical Access Hospitals?**

Three of the 14 were, according to the UNC Sheps Center tracker: Good Shepherd Medical Center in Linden, Hunt Regional Community Hospital of Commerce, and Little River Healthcare Rockdale Hospital. The study gives no separate result for Critical Access Hospitals.

**Q: Does the study apply to the smallest counties in West Texas and the Panhandle?**

No. It left out 79 counties that had fewer than 10 deaths from the four conditions in at least one year, and those counties averaged 4,439 people. The study has no finding for them either way.

**Q: Have studies outside Texas found the same thing?**

Some have. The authors say their findings match earlier studies that linked hospital closures to higher death rates in California and among Medicare patients. Other studies found no measurable change.
